  13. \olsection{Introduction}
  15. The completeness theorem is one of the most fundamental results about
  16. logic. It comes in two formulations, the equivalence of which we'll
  17. prove. In its first formulation it says something fundamental about
  18. the relationship between semantic consequence and our !!{derivation}
  19. system: if !!a{sentence}~$!A$ follows from some !!{sentence}s
  20. $\Gamma$, then there is also !!a{derivation} that establishes $\Gamma
  21. \Proves !A$. Thus, the !!{derivation} system is as strong as it can
  22. possibly be without proving things that don't actually follow.
  24. In its second formulation, it can be stated as a model existence
  25. result: every consistent set of !!{sentence}s is satisfiable.
  26. Consistency is a proof-theoretic notion: it says that our
  27. !!{derivation} system is unable to produce certain !!{derivation}s.
  28. But who's to say that just because there are no !!{derivation}s of a
  29. certain sort from~$\Gamma$, it's guaranteed that there is
  30. \iftag{FOL}{!!a{structure}~$\Struct{M}$}{!!{valuation}{~$\pAssign{v}$}
  31. with $\iftag{FOL}{\Sat{M}}{\pSat{v}}{\Gamma}$}? Before the
  32. completeness theorem was first proved---in fact before we had the
  33. !!{derivation} systems we now do---the great German mathematician
  34. David Hilbert held the view that consistency of mathematical theories
  35. guarantees the existence of the objects they are about. He put it as
  36. follows in a letter to Gottlob Frege:
  37. \begin{quote}
  38. If the arbitrarily given axioms do not contradict one another with
  39. all their consequences, then they are true and the things defined by
  40. the axioms exist. This is for me the criterion of truth and
  41. existence.
  42. \end{quote}
  43. Frege vehemently disagreed. The second formulation of the completeness
  44. theorem shows that Hilbert was right in at least the sense that if the
  45. axioms are consistent, then \emph{some}
  46. \iftag{FOL}{!!{structure}}{!!{valuation}} exists that makes them all
  47. true.
  49. These aren't the only reasons the completeness theorem---or rather,
  50. its proof---is important. It has a number of important consequences,
  51. some of which we'll discuss separately. For instance, since any
  52. !!{derivation} that shows $\Gamma \Proves !A$ is finite and so can
  53. only use finitely many of the !!{sentence}s in~$\Gamma$, it follows by
  54. the completeness theorem that if $!A$ is a consequence of~$\Gamma$, it
  55. is already a consequence of a finite subset of~$\Gamma$. This is
  56. called \emph{compactness}. Equivalently, if every finite subset of
  57. $\Gamma$ is consistent, then $\Gamma$ itself must be consistent.
  59. Although the compactness theorem follows from the completeness theorem
  60. via the detour through !!{derivation}s, it is also possible to use the
  61. \emph{the proof of} the completeness theorem to establish it
  62. directly. For what the proof does is take a set of !!{sentence}s with
  63. a certain property---consistency---and constructs !!a{structure} out
  64. of this set that has certain properties (in this case, that it
  65. satisfies the set). Almost the very same construction can be used to
  66. directly establish compactness, by starting from ``finitely
  67. satisfiable'' sets of !!{sentence}s instead of consistent ones.
  68. \iftag{FOL}{The construction also yields other consequences, e.g.,
  69. that any satisfiable set of !!{sentence}s has a finite or
  70. !!{denumerable}s model. (This result is called the
  71. L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem.) In general, the construction of
  72. !!{structure}s from sets of !!{sentence}s is used often in logic,
  73. and sometimes even in philosophy.}{}
  75. \end{document}

  15. The proof of the completeness theorem is a bit complex, and upon first
  16. reading it, it is easy to get lost. So let us outline the proof. The
  17. first step is a shift of perspective, that allows us to see a route to
  18. a proof. When completeness is thought of as ``whenever $\Gamma
  19. \Entails !A$ then $\Gamma \Proves !A$,'' it may be hard to even come
  20. up with an idea: for to show that $\Gamma \Proves !A$ we have to find
  21. !!a{derivation}, and it does not look like the hypothesis that
  22. $\Gamma \Entails !A$ helps us for this in any way. For some proof
  23. systems it is possible to directly construct !!a{derivation}, but we
  24. will take a slightly different approach. The shift in perspective
  25. required is this: completeness can also be formulated as: ``if
  26. $\Gamma$ is consistent, it is satisfiable.'' Perhaps we can use the
  27. information in~$\Gamma$ together with the hypothesis that it is
  28. consistent to construct \iftag{FOL}{!!a{structure}}{!!a{valuation}}
  29. that satisfies every \iftag{FOL}{!!{sentence}}{!!{formula}}
  30. in~$\Gamma$. After all, we know
  31. what kind of \iftag{FOL}{!!{structure}}{!!{valuation}} we are looking
  32. for: one that is as $\Gamma$ describes it!{}
  34. If $\Gamma$ contains only \iftag{FOL}{atomic
  35. !!{sentence}s}{!!{propositional variable}s}, it is easy to construct
  36. a model for it.\iftag{FOL}{ Suppose the atomic !!{sentence}s are all
  37. of the form $\Atom{P}{a_1,\dots,a_n}$ where the $a_i$ are
  38. !!{constant}s.}{} All we have to do is come up with
  39. \iftag{FOL}{%
  40. !!a{domain}~$\Domain{M}$ and an assignment for~$P$ so that
  41. $\Sat{M}{\Atom{P}{a_1,\ldots,a_n}}$. But that's not very hard:
  42. put $\Domain{M} = \Nat$, $\Assign{\Obj c_i}{M} = i$, and for every
  43. $\Atom{P}{a_1,\ldots,a_n} \in \Gamma$, put the tuple $\tuple{k_1,
  44. \dots, k_n}$ into $\Assign{P}{M}$, where $k_i$ is the index of the
  45. constant symbol~$a_i$ (i.e., $a_i \ident \Obj c_{k_i}$).}{%
  46. !!a{valuation}~$\pAssign{v}$ such that $\pSat{v}{p}$ for all $p \in
  47. \Gamma$. Well, let $\pAssign{v}(p) = \True$ iff $p \in \Gamma$.}
  49. Now suppose $\Gamma$ contains some !!{formula}~$\lnot !B$, with $!B$
  50. atomic. We might worry that the construction of
  51. $\iftag{FOL}{\Struct{M}}{\pAssign{v}}$ interferes with the possibility
  52. of making $\lnot !B$ true. But here's where the consistency
  53. of~$\Gamma$ comes in: if $\lnot !B \in \Gamma$, then $!B \notin
  54. \Gamma$, or else $\Gamma$ would be inconsistent. And if $!B \notin
  55. \Gamma$, then according to our construction
  56. of~$\iftag{FOL}{\Struct{M}}{\pAssign{v}}$,
  57. $\iftag{FOL}{\Sat/{M}}{\pSat/{v}}{!B}$, so
  58. $\iftag{FOL}{\Sat{M}}{\pSat{v}}{\lnot !B}$. So far so good.
  60. What if $\Gamma$ contains complex, non-atomic formulas? Say it
  61. contains $!A \land !B$. To make that true, we should proceed as if
  62. both $!A$ and $!B$ were in~$\Gamma$. And if $!A \lor !B \in \Gamma$,
  63. then we will have to make at least one of them true, i.e., proceed as
  64. if one of them was in~$\Gamma$.
  66. This suggests the following idea: we add additional !!{formula}s
  67. to~$\Gamma$ so as to (a)~keep the resulting set consistent and
  68. (b)~make sure that for every possible atomic !!{sentence}~$!A$, either
  69. $!A$ is in the resulting set, or $\lnot !A$ is, and (c)~such that,
  70. whenever $!A \land !B$ is in the set, so are both $!A$ and $!B$, if
  71. $!A \lor !B$ is in the set, at least one of $!A$ or $!B$ is also, etc.
  72. We keep doing this (potentially forever). Call the set of all
  73. !!{formula}s so added~$\Gamma^*$. Then our construction above would
  74. provide us with \iftag{FOL}{!!a{structure}~$\Struct{M}$}{!!a{valuation}~$\pAssign{v}$}
  75. for which we could prove, by induction, that it satisfies all sentences
  76. in~$\Gamma^*$, and hence also all sentence in~$\Gamma$
  77. since $\Gamma \subseteq \Gamma^*$. It turns out that guaranteeing (a)
  78. and~(b) is enough. A set of sentences for which (b) holds is called
  79. \emph{complete}. So our task will be to extend the consistent
  80. set~$\Gamma$ to a consistent and complete set~$\Gamma^*$.
  82. \iftag{FOL}{%

  84. we would hope to be able to pick some !!{constant}~$c$ and add $!A(c)$
  85. in this process. But how do we know we can always do that? Perhaps we
  86. only have a few !!{constant}s in our language, and for each one of
  87. them we have $\lnot !A(c) \in \Gamma$. We can't also add $!A(c)$,
  88. since this would make the set inconsistent, and we wouldn't know
  89. whether $\Struct{M}$ has to make $!A(c)$ or $\lnot !A(c)$ true.

  108. there is an obvious assignment of functions (that take terms as
  109. arguments and have terms as values) to !!{function}s: we assign to the

  115. !!{predicate}~$\eq$ has a fixed interpretation: $\Sat{M}{\eq[t][t']}$

  118. will make \emph{no} sentence of the form $\eq[t][t']$ true
  119. unless $t$ and $t'$ are one and the same term. And of
  120. course this is a problem, since basically every interesting theory in
  121. a language with !!{function}s will have as theorems sentences
  122. $\eq[t][t']$ where $t$ and $t'$ are not the same term (e.g., in
  123. theories of arithmetic: $\eq[(\Obj 0+ \Obj 0)][\Obj 0]$). To solve
  124. this problem, we change the domain of~$\Struct M$: instead of using terms as
  125. the objects in~$\Domain{M}$, we use sets of terms, and each set is so
  126. that it contains all those terms which the sentences in~$\Gamma$
  127. require to be equal. So, e.g., if $\Gamma$ is a theory of arithmetic,
  128. one of these sets will contain: $\Obj 0$, $(\Obj 0 + \Obj 0)$, $(\Obj
  129. 0 \times \Obj 0)$, etc. This will be the set we assign to $\Obj 0$,
  130. and it will turn out that this set is also the value of all the terms
  131. in it, e.g., also of $(\Obj 0 + \Obj 0)$. Therefore, the sentence
  132. $\eq[(\Obj 0+ \Obj 0)][\Obj 0]$ will be true in this revised
  133. !!{structure}.}{}

  136. !!{complete} consistent sets, in particular we prove that
  137. !!a{complete} consistent set contains $!A \land !B$ iff it contains
  138. both $!A$ and~$!B$, $!A \lor !B$ iff it contains at least one of them,
  139. etc. (\olref[ccs]{prop:ccs}).\iftag{FOL}{ Then we define and

  148. }\iftag{prvAll}{$\lforall[x][!A(x)]$ iff it contains $!A(t)$ for all

  19. \begin{defn}[Complete set]
  20. \ollabel{def:complete-set} A set~$\Gamma$ of !!{sentence}s is
  21. \emph{!!{complete}} iff for any !!{sentence}~$!A$, either $!A \in
  22. \Gamma$ or $\lnot !A \in \Gamma$.
  23. \end{defn}
  25. \begin{explain}
  26. !!^{complete} sets of sentences leave no questions unanswered. For
  27. any !!{sentence}~$!A$, $\Gamma$ ``says'' if $!A$ is true or false. The
  28. importance of !!{complete} sets extends beyond the proof of the
  29. completeness theorem. A theory which is !!{complete} and
  30. axiomatizable, for instance, is always decidable.
  31. \end{explain}
  33. \begin{explain}
  34. !!^{complete} consistent sets are important in the completeness proof
  35. since we can guarantee that every consistent set of
  36. !!{sentence}s~$\Gamma$ is contained in !!a{complete} consistent
  37. set~$\Gamma^*$. !!^a{complete} consistent set contains, for each
  38. !!{sentence}~$!A$, either $!A$ or its negation $\lnot !A$, but not
  39. both. This is true in particular for \iftag{FOL}{atomic
  40. !!{sentence}s}{propositional variables}, so from !!a{complete}
  41. consistent set\iftag{FOL}{ in a language suitably expanded by
  42. !!{constant}s}{}, we can construct
  43. \iftag{FOL}{!!a{structure}}{!!a{valuation}} where the
  44. \iftag{FOL}{interpretation of !!{predicate}s}{truth value assigned to
  45. propositional variables} is defined according to which
  46. \iftag{FOL}{atomic !!{sentence}s}{!!{propositional variable}s} are
  47. in~$\Gamma^*$. This \iftag{FOL}{!!{structure}}{!!{valuation}} can then
  48. be shown to make all !!{sentence}s in~$\Gamma^*$ (and hence also all
  49. those in~$\Gamma$) true. The proof of this latter fact requires that
  50. $\lnot !A \in \Gamma^*$ iff $!A \notin \Gamma^*$, $(!A \lor !B) \in
  51. \Gamma^*$ iff $!A \in \Gamma^*$ or $!B \in \Gamma^*$, etc.

  15. \begin{explain}

  17. !!{sentence}s is contained in some set of sentences which is not just
  18. consistent, but also !!{complete}. The proof works by adding one
  19. !!{sentence} at a time, guaranteeing at each step that the set remains
  20. consistent. We do this so that for every $!A$, either $!A$ or $\lnot
  21. !A$ gets added at some stage. The union of all stages in that
  22. construction then contains either $!A$ or its negation~$\lnot !A$ and
  23. is thus complete. It is also consistent, since we make sure at each
  24. stage not to introduce an inconsistency.
  25. \end{explain}
  27. \begin{lem}[Lindenbaum's Lemma]
  28. \ollabel{lem:lindenbaum} Every consistent
  29. set~$\Gamma$ in a language~$\Lang{L}$ can be
  30. extended to !!a{complete} and consistent set~$\Gamma^*$.
  31. \end{lem}

  34. Let $\Gamma$ be consistent. Let $!A_0$, $!A_1$,
  35. \dots{} be an enumeration of all the !!{sentence}s of~$\Lang L$.

  40. \Gamma_n \cup \{ !A_n \} & \textrm{if $\Gamma_n \cup \{!A_n\}$ is
  41. consistent;} \\
  42. \Gamma_n \cup \{ \lnot !A_n \} & \textrm{otherwise.}
  43. \end{cases}

  49. is consistent. If it isn't, $\Gamma_{n+1} = \Gamma_n \cup \{\lnot
  50. !A_n\}$. We have to verify that $\Gamma_n \cup \{\lnot !A_n\}$ is
  51. consistent. Suppose it's not. Then \emph{both} $\Gamma_n \cup
  52. \{!A_n\}$ and $\Gamma_n \cup \{\lnot !A_n\}$ are inconsistent. This
  53. means that $\Gamma_n$ would be inconsistent by

  10. \olsection{The L\"owenheim--Skolem Theorem}
  12. The L\"owenheim--Skolem Theorem says that if a theory has an infinite
  13. model, then it also has a model that is at most !!{denumerable}. An
  14. immediate consequence of this fact is that first-order logic cannot
  15. express that the size of !!a{structure} is !!{nonenumerable}: any
  16. !!{sentence} or set of !!{sentence}s satisfied in all
  17. !!{nonenumerable} !!{structure}s is also satisfied in some
  18. !!{enumerable} structure.
  20. \begin{thm}
  21. \ollabel{thm:downward-ls} If $\Gamma$ is consistent then it has
  22. !!a{enumerable} model, i.e., it is satisfiable in !!a{structure}
  23. whose domain is either finite or !!{denumerable}.
  24. \end{thm}
  26. \begin{proof}
  27. If $\Gamma$ is consistent, the !!{structure}~$\Struct M$ delivered by
  28. the proof of the completeness theorem has a domain $\Domain{M}$ that
  29. is no larger than the set of the terms of the language~$\Lang L$. So
  30. $\Struct M$ is at most !!{denumerable}.
  31. \end{proof}
  33. \begin{thm}
  34. \ollabel{noidentity-ls} If $\Gamma$ is a consistent set of !!{sentence}s
  35. in the language of first-order logic without identity, then it has
  36. !!a{denumerable} model, i.e., it is satisfiable in !!a{structure}
  37. whose domain is infinite and !!{enumerable}.
  38. \end{thm}
  40. \begin{proof}
  41. If $\Gamma$ is consistent and contains no sentences in which identity
  42. appears, then the !!{structure}~$\Struct M$ delivered by the proof of
  43. the completeness theorem has a domain $\Domain{M}$ identical to the set
  44. of terms of the language~$\Lang L'$. So $\Struct{M}$ is
  45. !!{denumerable}, since $\Trm[L']$ is.
  46. \end{proof}
  48. \begin{ex}[Skolem's Paradox]
  49. Zermelo--Fraenkel set theory~$\Log{ZFC}$ is a very powerful framework
  50. in which practically all mathematical statements can be expressed,
  51. including facts about the sizes of sets. So for instance, $\Log{ZFC}$
  52. can prove that the set~$\Real$ of real numbers is !!{nonenumerable},
  53. it can prove Cantor's Theorem that the power set of any set is larger
  54. than the set itself, etc. If $\Log{ZFC}$ is consistent, its models
  55. are all infinite, and moreover, they all contain !!{element}s about
  56. which the theory says that they are !!{nonenumerable}, such as the
  57. element that makes true the theorem of~$\Log{ZFC}$ that the power set
  58. of the natural numbers exists. By the L\"owenheim--Skolem Theorem,
  59. $\Log{ZFC}$ also has !!{enumerable} models---models that contain
  60. ``!!{nonenumerable}'' sets but which themselves are !!{enumerable}.
  61. \end{ex}
