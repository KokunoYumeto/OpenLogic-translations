  11. \olsection{Syntax}
  13. We first must make precise what strings of symbols count as
  14. !!{sentence}s of first-order logic. We'll do this later; for now
  15. we'll just proceed by example. The basic building blocks---the
  16. vocabulary---of first-order logic divides into two parts. The first
  17. part is the symbols we use to say specific things or to pick out
  18. specific things. We pick out things using !!{constant}s, and we say
  19. stuff about the things we pick out using !!{predicate}s. E.g, we
  20. might use $\Obj a$ as !!a{constant} to pick out a single thing, and
  21. then say something about it using the !!{sentence}~$\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj
  22. a}$. If you have meanings for ``$\Obj a$'' and ``$\Obj P$'' in mind,
  23. you can read $\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj a}$ as a sentence of English (and you
  24. probably have done so when you first learned formal logic). Once you
  25. have such simple !!{sentence}s of first-order logic, you can build
  26. more complex ones using the second part of the vocabulary: the logical
  27. symbols (connectives and quantifiers). So, for instance, we can form
  28. expressions like $(\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj a} \land \Atom{\Obj Q}{\Obj b})$
  29. or~$\lexists[\Obj x][\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj x}]$.
  31. In order to provide the precise definitions of semantics and the rules
  32. of our !!{derivation} systems required for rigorous meta-logical study, we
  33. first of all have to give a precise definition of what counts as
  34. !!a{sentence} of first-order logic. The basic idea is easy enough to
  35. understand: there are some simple !!{sentence}s we can form from just
  36. !!{predicate}s and !!{constant}s, such as~$\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj a}$. And
  37. then from these we form more complex ones using the connectives and
  38. quantifiers. But what exactly are the rules by which we are allowed to
  39. form more complex !!{sentence}s? These must be specified, otherwise
  40. we have not defined ``!!{sentence} of first-order logic'' precisely
  41. enough. There are a few issues. The first one is to get the right
  42. strings to count as !!{sentence}s. The second one is to do this in
  43. such a way that we can give mathematical proofs about \emph{all}
  44. !!{sentence}s. Finally, we'll have to also give precise definitions of
  45. some rudimentary operations with !!{sentence}s, such as ``replace
  46. every $\Obj x$ in~$!A$ by~$\Obj b$.'' The trouble is that the
  47. quantifiers and !!{variable}s we have in first-order logic make it not

  17. role !!{variable}s play in them---and on the basis of some other

  19. define what !!a{sentence} is (namely, !!a{formula} without free
  20. !!{variable}s). We do this not just because it makes the definition of
  21. ``!!{sentence}'' more manageable, but also because it will be crucial
  22. to the way we define the semantic notion of satisfaction.

  25. containing only a single !!{predicate}~$\Obj P$ and a single
  26. !!{constant}~$\Obj a$, and only the logical symbols $\lnot$, $\land$,
  27. and~$\lexists$. Our full definitions will be much more general:

  29. we will also consider !!{function}s which can be combined with
  30. !!{constant}s and !!{variable}s to form ``terms.'' For now, $\Obj a$
  31. and the variables will be our only terms. We do need infinitely many

  33. v_1$, \dots, as variables.

  34. that satisfies all the !!{sentence}s in~$\Gamma$ also satisfies~$!A$.

  36. satisfies all !!{sentence}s in it at the same time.

  39. turn defined by induction on the structure of !!{formula}s, we can use
  40. induction to prove properties of our semantics and to relate the

  42. properties, partly because they are individually interesting, but
  43. mainly because many of them will come in handy when we go on to

  13. Once we've defined the syntax and semantics of first-order logic, we
  14. can get to work investigating the properties of !!{structure}s and the
  15. semantic notions. We can also define !!{derivation} systems, and
  16. investigate those. For a set of !!{sentence}s, we can ask: what
  17. !!{structure}s make all the !!{sentence}s in that set true? Given a
  18. set of !!{sentence}s~$\Gamma$, !!a{structure}~$\Struct{M}$ that
  19. satisfies them is called a \emph{model of~$\Gamma$}. We might start
  20. from~$\Gamma$ and try to find its models---what do they look like? How
  21. big or small do they have to be? But we might also start with a single
  22. !!{structure} or collection of !!{structure}s and ask: what
  23. !!{sentence}s are true in them? Are there !!{sentence}s that
  24. \emph{characterize} these !!{structure}s in the sense that they, and
  25. only they, are true in them? These kinds of questions are the domain
  26. of \emph{model theory}. They also underlie the \emph{axiomatic
  27. method}: describing a collection of !!{structure}s by a set of
  28. !!{sentence}s, the axioms of a theory. This is made possible by the
  29. observation that exactly those !!{sentence}s entailed in first-order
  30. logic by the axioms are true in all models of the axioms.
  32. As a very simple example, consider preorders. A preorder is a
  33. relation~$R$ on some set~$A$ which is both reflexive and transitive.
  34. A set~$A$ with a two-place relation $R \subseteq A \times A$ on it is
  35. exactly what we would need to give !!a{structure} for a first-order
  36. language with a single two-place relation symbol~$\Obj P$: we would
  37. set $\Domain{M} = A$ and $\Assign{\Obj P}{M} = R$. Since $R$~is a
  38. preorder, it is reflexive and transitive, and we can find a
  39. set~$\Gamma$ of !!{sentence}s of first-order logic that say this:
  40. \begin{align*}
  41. & \lforall[\Obj v_0][\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj v_0,\Obj v_0}]\\
  42. & \lforall[\Obj v_0][\lforall[\Obj v_1][\lforall[\Obj v_2][((\Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj v_0,\Obj v_1} \land \Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj v_1,\Obj v_2}) \lif \Atom{\Obj P}{\Obj v_0,\Obj v_2})]]]
  43. \end{align*}

  45. $Rxx$'' ($R$ is reflexive) and ``whenever $Rxy$ and $Ryz$ then also
  46. $Rxz$'' ($R$ is transitive). We see that !!a{structure}~$\Struct{M}$
  47. is a model of these two !!{sentence}s~$\Gamma$ iff $R$ (i.e.,

  50. property of all preorders that can be expressed in the first-order

  13. We'll also introduce !!{derivation} systems for first-order logic. There are
  14. many !!{derivation} systems that logicians have developed, but they all define
  15. the same !!{derivability} relation between !!{sentence}s. We say that
  16. $\Gamma$ \emph{!!{derive}s}~$!A$, $\Gamma \Proves !A$, if there is
  17. !!a{derivation} of a certain precisely defined sort. !!^{derivation}s
  18. are always finite arrangements of symbols---perhaps a list of
  19. !!{sentence}s, or some more complicated structure. The purpose of
  20. !!{derivation} systems is to provide a tool to determine if !!a{sentence} is
  21. entailed by some set~$\Gamma$. In order to serve that purpose, it
  22. must be true that $\Gamma \Entails !A$ if, and only if, $\Gamma
  23. \Proves !A$.
  25. If $\Gamma \Proves !A$ but not $\Gamma \Entails !A$, our !!{derivation} system
  26. would be too strong, prove too much. The property that if $\Gamma
  27. \Proves !A$ then $\Gamma \Entails !A$ is called \emph{soundness}, and
  28. it is a minimal requirement on any good !!{derivation} system. On the other
  29. hand, if $\Gamma \Entails !A$ but not $\Gamma \Proves !A$, then our
  30. !!{derivation} system is too weak, it doesn't prove enough. The property that
  31. if $\Gamma \Entails !A$ then $\Gamma \Proves !A$ is called
  32. \emph{completeness}. Soundness is usually relatively easy to prove (by
  33. induction on the structure of !!{derivation}s, which are inductively
  34. defined). Completeness is harder to prove.
  36. Soundness and completeness have a number of important consequences. If
  37. a set of !!{sentence}s~$\Gamma$ !!{derive}s a contradiction (such as
  38. $!A \land \lnot !A$) it is called \emph{inconsistent}. Inconsistent
  39. $\Gamma$s cannot have any models, they are unsatisfiable. From
  40. completeness the converse follows: any $\Gamma$ that is not
  41. inconsistent---or, as we will say, \emph{consistent}---has a model. In
  42. fact, this is equivalent to completeness, and is the form of
  43. completeness we will actually prove. It is a deep and perhaps
  44. surprising result: just because you cannot prove $!A \land \lnot !A$
  45. from $\Gamma$ guarantees that there is !!a{structure} that is as
  46. $\Gamma$ describes it. So completeness gives an answer to the
  47. question: which sets of !!{sentence}s have models? Answer: all and only
  48. consistent sets do.
  50. The soundness and completeness theorems have two important
  51. consequences: the compactness and the L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem.
  52. These are important results in the theory of models, and can be used
  53. to establish many interesting results. We've already mentioned two:
  54. first-order logic cannot express that the !!{domain} of !!a{structure}
  55. is finite or that it is !!{nonenumerable}.
