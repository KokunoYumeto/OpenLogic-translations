Section. Rules and Derivations Explanation. Natural deduction systems are meant to closely parallel the informal reasoning used in mathematical proof (hence it is somewhat “natural”). Natural deduction proofs begin with assumptions. Inference rules are then applied. Assumptions are “discharged” by the negation introduction, conditional introduction, disjunction elimination and existential quantifier elimination inference rules, and the label of the discharged assumption is placed beside the inference for clarity. Definition 1: Assumption. An assumption is any sentence in the topmost position of any branch. Derivations in natural deduction are certain trees of sentences, where the topmost sentences are assumptions, and if a sentence stands below one, two, or three other sequents, it must follow correctly by a rule of inference. The sentences at the top of the inference are called the premises, and the sentence below is called the conclusion of the inference. Reader correction: the source omits “is called” in the second clause. The rules come in pairs, an introduction and an elimination rule for each logical operator. They introduce a logical operator in the conclusion or remove a logical operator from a premise of the rule. Some of the rules allow an assumption of a certain type to be discharged. To indicate which assumption is discharged by which inference, we also assign labels to both the assumption and the inference. This is indicated by writing the assumption as “ assumption A, labeled n for discharge .” It is customary to consider rules for all the logical operators conjunction , disjunction , conditional , negation , and a contradiction , even if some of those are defined.

Section. Derivations Explanation. We've said what an assumption is, and we've given the rules of inference. Derivations in natural deduction are inductively generated from these: each derivation either is an assumption on its own, or consists of one, two, or three derivations followed by a correct inference. Definition 9: Derivation. A derivation of a sentence formula A from assumptions Gamma is a finite tree of sentences satisfying the following conditions: Item 1. The topmost sentences of the tree are either in Gamma or are discharged by an inference in the tree. Item 2. The bottommost sentence of the tree is formula A . Item 3. Every sentence in the tree except the sentence formula A at the bottom is a premise of a correct application of an inference rule whose conclusion stands directly below that sentence in the tree. We then say that formula A is the conclusion of the derivation and Gamma its undischarged assumptions. If a derivation of formula A from Gamma exists, we say that formula A is derivable from Gamma , or in symbols: Gamma syntactically derives A . If there is a derivation of formula A in which every assumption is discharged, we write A is derivable without undischarged assumptions . Example 1. Every assumption on its own is a derivation. So, e.g., formula A by itself is a derivation, and so is formula B by itself. We can obtain a new derivation from these by applying, say, the conjunction introduction rule, Step 1 states formula A as a premise. Step 2 states formula B as a premise. Section. Soundness Explanation. A Derivation system, such as natural deduction, is sound if it cannot derive things that do not actually follow. Soundness is thus a kind of guaranteed safety property for derivation systems. Depending on which proof theoretic property is in question, we would like to know for instance, that Item 1. every derivable sentence is valid; Item 2. if a sentence is derivable from some others, it is also a consequence of them; Item 3. if a set of sentences is inconsistent, it is unsatisfiable. These are important properties of a derivation system. If any of them do not hold, the derivation system is deficient—it would derive too much.
