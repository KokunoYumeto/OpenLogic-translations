Introduction

Giving the meaning of expressions is the domain of semantics. The central concept in semantics is that of satisfaction in a structure. A structure gives meaning to the building blocks of the language: a domain is a non-empty set of objects. The quantifiers are interpreted as ranging over this domain, constants are assigned elements in the domain, functions are assigned functions from the domain to itself, and predicates are assigned relations on the domain. The domain together with assignments to the basic vocabulary constitutes a structure. Variables may appear in formulas, and in order to give a semantics, we also have to assign elements of the domain to them—this is a variable assignment. The satisfaction relation, finally, brings these together. A formula may be satisfied in a structure M relative to a variable assignment s, written as structure M under assignment s satisfies A. This relation is also defined by induction on the structure of formula A, using the truth tables for the logical connectives to define, say, satisfaction of open scope formula A and formula B close scope in terms of satisfaction (or not) of formula A and formula B. It then turns out that the variable assignment is irrelevant if the formula A is a sentence, that is, has no free variables, and so we can talk of sentences being simply satisfied (or not) in structures.

On the basis of the satisfaction relation structure M satisfies A for sentences we can then define the basic semantic notions of validity, entailment, and satisfiability. A sentence is valid, A is valid, if every structure satisfies it. It is entailed by a set of sentences, Gamma semantically entails A, if every structure that satisfies all the sentences in Gamma also satisfies formula A. And a set of sentences is satisfiable if some structure satisfies all sentences in it at the same time. Because formulas are inductively defined, and satisfaction is in turn defined by induction on the structure of formulas, we can use induction to prove properties of our semantics and to relate the semantic notions defined.

structure for First-order Languages

Explanation

First-order languages are, by themselves, uninterpreted: the constants, functions, and predicates have no specific meaning attached to them. Meanings are given by specifying a structure. It specifies the domain, that is, the objects which the constants pick out, the functions operate on, and the quantifiers range over. In addition, it specifies which constants pick out which objects, how a function maps objects to objects, and which objects the predicates apply to. Structures are the basis for semantic notions in logic, for example, the notion of consequence, validity, satisfiability. They are variously called “structures,” “interpretations,” or “models” in the literature.

Satisfaction of a formula in a structure

Explanation

The basic notion that relates expressions such as terms and formulas, on the one hand, and structures on the other, are those of value of a term and satisfaction of a formula. Informally, the value of a term is an element of a structure—if the term is just a constant, its value is the object assigned to the constant by the structure, and if it is built up using functions, the value is computed from the values of constants and the functions assigned to the functions in the term. A formula is satisfied in a structure if the interpretation given to the predicates makes the formula true in the domain of the structure. This notion of satisfaction is specified inductively: the specification of the structure directly states when atomic formulas are satisfied, and we define when a complex formula is satisfied depending on the main connective or quantifier and whether or not the immediate subformulas are satisfied.

The case of the quantifiers here is a bit tricky, as the immediate subformula of a quantified formula has a free variable, and structures don't specify the values of variables. In order to deal with this difficulty, we also introduce variable assignments and define satisfaction not with respect to a structure alone, but with respect to a structure plus a variable assignment.

Variable Assignments

Explanation

A variable assignment s provides a value for every variable—and there are infinitely many of them. This is of course not necessary. We require variable assignments to assign values to all variables simply because it makes things a lot easier. The value of a term t, and whether or not a formula A is satisfied in a structure with respect to variable assignment s, only depend on the assignments s makes to the variables in term t and the free variables of formula A. Sentence satisfaction is assignment-independent

If A is a sentence and s a variable assignment, then structure M under assignment s satisfies A if and only if structure M under assignment s prime satisfies A for every variable assignment s prime.

Proof

Let s prime be any variable assignment. Since A is a sentence, it has no free variables, and so every variable assignment s prime trivially assigns the same things to all free variables of sentence A as does variable assignment s. So the condition of Satisfaction independence proposition is satisfied, and we have structure M under assignment s satisfies A if and only if structure M under assignment s prime satisfies A.

End of proof.

Extensionality

Explanation

Extensionality, sometimes called relevance, can be expressed informally as follows: the only factors that bear upon the satisfaction of formula A in a structure M relative to a variable assignment s, are the size of the domain and the assignments made by structure M and variable assignment s to the elements of the language that actually appear in formula A.

One immediate consequence of extensionality is that where two structures M and M prime agree on all the elements of the language appearing in a sentence A and have the same domain, structure M and structure M prime must also agree on whether or not formula A itself is true.

Extensionality proposition

Source title: Extensionality.

Let A be a formula, and M sub one and M sub two be structures with the domain of structure M sub one equals the domain of structure M sub two, and s a variable assignment on the domain of structure M sub one equals the domain of structure M sub two. If the interpretation of c in structure M sub one equals the interpretation of c in structure M sub two, the interpretation of R in structure M sub one equals the interpretation of R in structure M sub two, and the interpretation of f in structure M sub one equals the interpretation of f in structure M sub two for every constant c, relation symbol R, and function f occurring in formula A, then structure M sub one under assignment s satisfies A if and only if structure M sub two under assignment s satisfies A.

Proof

First prove (by induction on term t) that for every term, the value of t in structure M sub one under assignment s equals the value of t in structure M sub two under assignment s. Then prove the proposition by induction on formula A, making use of the claim just proved for the induction basis (where formula A is atomic).

End of proof.

Semantic Notions

Explanation

Given the definition of structures for first-order languages, we can define some basic semantic properties of and relationships between sentences. The simplest of these is the notion of validity of a sentence. A sentence is valid if it is satisfied in every structure. Valid sentences are those that are satisfied regardless of how the non-logical symbols in it are interpreted. Valid sentences are therefore also called logical truths—they are true, that is, satisfied, in any structure and hence their truth depends only on the logical symbols occurring in them and their syntactic structure, but not on the non-logical symbols or their interpretation.

Definition of validity

Source title: Validity.

A sentence A is valid, A is valid, if and only if structure M satisfies A for every structure M.

Definition of semantic entailment

Source title: Entailment.

A set of sentences Gamma entails a sentence A, Gamma semantically entails A, if and only if for every structure M with structure M satisfies Gamma, structure M satisfies A.

Definition of satisfiability

Source title: Satisfiability.

A set of sentences Gamma is satisfiable if structure M satisfies Gamma for some structure M. If Gamma is not satisfiable it is called unsatisfiable.

Validity as universal entailment

A sentence A is valid if and only if Gamma semantically entails A for every set of sentences Gamma.

Proof

For the forward direction, let sentence A be valid, and let Gamma be a set of sentences. Let M be a structure so that structure M satisfies Gamma. Since sentence A is valid, structure M satisfies A, hence Gamma semantically entails A.

For the contrapositive of the reverse direction, let sentence A be invalid, so there is a structure M with structure M does not satisfy A. When Gamma equals open brace verum close brace, since truth constant is valid, structure M satisfies Gamma. Hence, there is a structure M so that structure M satisfies Gamma but structure M does not satisfy A, hence Gamma does not entail sentence A.

End of proof.

Entailment by unsatisfiability

Gamma semantically entails A if and only if Gamma union the singleton containing not A is unsatisfiable.

Proof

For the forward direction, suppose Gamma semantically entails A and suppose to the contrary that there is a structure M so that structure M satisfies Gamma union open brace not A close brace. Since structure M satisfies Gamma and Gamma semantically entails A, structure M satisfies A. Also, since structure M satisfies Gamma union open brace not A close brace, structure M satisfies not A, so we have both structure M satisfies A and structure M does not satisfy A, a contradiction. Hence, there can be no such structure M, so Gamma union the singleton containing not A is unsatisfiable.

For the reverse direction, suppose Gamma union the singleton containing not A is unsatisfiable. So for every structure M, either structure M does not satisfy Gamma or structure M satisfies A. Hence, for every structure M with structure M satisfies Gamma, structure M satisfies A, so Gamma semantically entails A.

End of proof.
