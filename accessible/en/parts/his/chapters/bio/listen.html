Chapter. Biographies.

Section. Georg Cantor. An early biography of Georg Cantor ( gay -org kahn -tor) claimed that he was born and found on a ship that was sailing for Saint Petersburg, Russia, and that his parents were unknown. This, however, is not true; although he was born in Saint Petersburg in one thousand eight hundred forty five. Cantor received his doctorate in mathematics at the University of Berlin in one thousand eight hundred sixty seven. He is known for his work in set theory, and is credited with founding set theory as a distinctive research discipline. He was the first to prove that there are infinite sets of different sizes. His theories, and especially his theory of infinities, caused much debate among mathematicians at the time, and his work was controversial. Cantor's religious beliefs and his mathematical work were inextricably tied; he even claimed that the theory of transfinite numbers had been communicated to him directly by God. In later life, Cantor suffered from mental illness. Beginning in one thousand eight hundred ninety four, and more frequently towards his later years, Cantor was hospitalized. The heavy criticism of his work, including a falling out with the mathematician Leopold Kronecker, led to depression and a lack of interest in mathematics. During depressive episodes, Cantor would turn to philosophy and literature, and even published a theory that Francis Bacon was the author of Shakespeare's plays. Cantor died on January six, one thousand nine hundred eighteen, in a sanatorium in Halle. Further reading. For full biographies of Cantor, see Joseph Dauben (one thousand nine hundred ninety) and Ivor Grattan-Guinness (one thousand nine hundred seventy one) . Cantor's radical views are also described in the BBC Radio four program A Brief History of Mathematics (Marcus du Sautoy, two thousand fourteen) . If you'd like to hear about Cantor's theories in rap form, see Daniel Rose (two thousand twelve) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Alonzo Church. Alonzo Church was born in Washington, DC on June fourteen, one thousand nine hundred three. In early childhood, an air gun incident left Church blind in one eye. He finished preparatory school in Connecticut in one thousand nine hundred twenty and began his university education at Princeton that same year. He completed his doctoral studies in one thousand nine hundred twenty seven. After a couple years abroad, Church returned to Princeton. Church was known to be exceedingly polite and careful. His blackboard writing was immaculate, and he would preserve important papers by carefully covering them in Duco cement (a clear glue). Outside of his academic pursuits, he enjoyed reading science fiction magazines and was not afraid to write to the editors if he spotted any inaccuracies in the writing. Church's academic achievements were great. Together with his students Stephen Kleene and Barkley Rosser, he developed a theory of effective calculability, the lambda calculus, independently of Alan Turing's development of the Turing machine. The two definitions of computability are equivalent, and give rise to what is now known as the Church—Turing Thesis, that a function of the natural numbers is effectively computable if and only if it is computable via Turing machine (or lambda calculus). He also proved what is now known as Church's Theorem: The decision problem for the validity of first-order formulas is unsolvable. Church continued his work into old age. In one thousand nine hundred sixty seven he left Princeton for UCLA, where he was professor until his retirement in one thousand nine hundred ninety. Church passed away on August one, one thousand nine hundred ninety five at the age of ninety two. Further reading. For a brief biography of Church, see Herbert B. Enderton (two thousand nineteen) . Church's original writings on the lambda calculus and the Entscheidungsproblem (Church's Thesis) are Alonzo Church (one thousand nine hundred thirty six) and Alonzo Church (one thousand nine hundred thirty six) . William Aspray (one thousand nine hundred eighty four) records an interview with Church about the Princeton mathematics community in the nineteen thirties. Church wrote a series of book reviews of the Journal of Symbolic Logic from one thousand nine hundred thirty six until one thousand nine hundred seventy nine. They are all archived on John MacFarlane's website (John MacFarlane, two thousand fifteen) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Gerhard Gentzen. Gerhard Gentzen is known primarily as the creator of structural proof theory, and specifically the creation of the natural deduction and sequent calculus derivation systems. He was born on November twenty four, one thousand nine hundred nine in Greifswald, Germany. Gerhard was homeschooled for three years before attending preparatory school, where he was behind most of his classmates in terms of education. Despite this, he was a brilliant student and showed a strong aptitude for mathematics. His interests were varied, and he, for instance, also write poems for his mother and plays for the school theatre. Gentzen began his university studies at the University of Greifswald, but moved around to Goettingen, Munich, and Berlin. He received his doctorate in one thousand nine hundred thirty three from the University of Goettingen under Hermann Weyl. (Paul Bernays supervised most of his work, but was dismissed from the university by the Nazis.) In one thousand nine hundred thirty four, Gentzen began work as an assistant to David Hilbert. That same year he developed the sequent calculus and natural deduction derivation systems, in his papers Untersuchungen ueber das logische Schließen I—II Translation. Investigations Into Logical Deduction, parts one and two. End translation.. He proved the consistency of the Peano axioms in one thousand nine hundred thirty six. Gentzen's relationship with the Nazis is complicated. At the same time his mentor Bernays was forced to leave Germany, Gentzen joined the university branch of the SA, the Nazi paramilitary organization. Like many Germans, he was a member of the Nazi party. During the war, he served as a telecommunications officer for the air intelligence unit. However, in one thousand nine hundred forty two he was released from duty due to a nervous breakdown. It is unclear whether or not Gentzen's loyalties lay with the Nazi party, or whether he joined the party in order to ensure academic success. In one thousand nine hundred forty three, Gentzen was offered an academic position at the Mathematical Institute of the German University of Prague, which he accepted. However, in one thousand nine hundred forty five the citizens of Prague revolted against German occupation. Soviet forces arrived in the city and arrested all the professors at the university. Because of his membership in Nazi organizations, Gentzen was taken to a forced labour camp. He died of malnutrition while in his cell on August four, one thousand nine hundred forty five at the age of thirty five. Further reading. For a full biography of Gentzen, see Eckart Menzler-Trott (two thousand seven) . An interesting read about mathematicians under Nazi rule, which gives a brief note about Gentzen's life, is given by Sanford L. Segal (two thousand fourteen) . Gentzen's papers on logical deduction are available in the original german (Gerhard Gentzen, one thousand nine hundred thirty five) and (Gerhard Gentzen, one thousand nine hundred thirty five) . English translations of Gentzen's papers have been collected in a single volume by Manfred E. Szabo (one thousand nine hundred sixty nine) , which also includes a biographical sketch. End further reading. End section.

Section. Kurt Goedel. Kurt Goedel ( ger -dle) was born on April twenty eight, one thousand nine hundred six in Bruenn in the Austro-Hungarian empire (now Brno in the Czech Republic). Due to his inquisitive and bright nature, young Kurtele was often called “Der kleine Herr Warum” (Little Mr. Why) by his family. He excelled in academics from primary school onward, where he got less than the highest grade only in mathematics. Goedel was often absent from school due to poor health and was exempt from physical education. He was diagnosed with rheumatic fever during his childhood. Throughout his life, he believed this permanently affected his heart despite medical assessment saying otherwise. Goedel began studying at the University of Vienna in one thousand nine hundred twenty four and completed his doctoral studies in one thousand nine hundred twenty nine. He first intended to study physics, but his interests soon moved to mathematics and especially logic, in part due to the influence of the philosopher Rudolf Carnap. His dissertation, written under the supervision of Hans Hahn, proved the completeness theorem of first-order predicate logic with identity (Kurt Gödel, one thousand nine hundred twenty nine) . Only a year later, he obtained his most famous results—the first and second incompleteness theorems (published in Kurt Gödel one thousand nine hundred thirty one ). During his time in Vienna, Goedel was heavily involved with the Vienna Circle, a group of scientifically-minded philosophers that included Carnap, whose work was especially influenced by Goedel's results. In one thousand nine hundred thirty eight, Goedel married Adele Nimbursky. His parents were not pleased: not only was she six years older than him and already divorced, but she worked as a dancer in a nightclub. Social pressures did not affect Goedel, however, and they remained happily married until his death. After Nazi Germany annexed Austria in one thousand nine hundred thirty eight, Goedel and Adele emigrated to the United States, where he took up a position at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey. Despite his introversion and eccentric nature, Goedel's time at Princeton was collaborative and fruitful. He published essays in set theory, philosophy and physics. Notably, he struck up a particularly strong friendship with his colleague at the IAS, Albert Einstein. In his later years, Goedel's mental health deteriorated. His wife's hospitalization in one thousand nine hundred seventy seven meant she was no longer able to cook his meals for him. Having suffered from mental health issues throughout his life, he succumbed to paranoia. Deathly afraid of being poisoned, Goedel refused to eat. He died of starvation on January fourteen, one thousand nine hundred seventy eight, in Princeton. Further reading. For a complete biography of Goedel's life is available, see John Dawson, Jr. (one thousand nine hundred ninety seven) . For further biographical pieces, as well as essays about Goedel's contributions to logic and philosophy, see Hao Wang (one thousand nine hundred ninety) , Matthias Baaz and Christos H. Section. Emmy Noether. Emmy Noether ( ner -ter) was born in Erlangen, Germany, on March twenty three, one thousand eight hundred eighty two, to an upper-middle class scholarly family. Hailed as the “mother of modern algebra,” Noether made groundbreaking contributions to both mathematics and physics, despite significant barriers to women's education. In Germany at the time, young girls were meant to be educated in arts and were not allowed to attend college preparatory schools. However, after auditing classes at the Universities of Goettingen and Erlangen (where her father was professor of mathematics), Noether was eventually able to enroll as a student at Erlangen in one thousand nine hundred four, when their policy was updated to allow female students. She received her doctorate in mathematics in one thousand nine hundred seven. Despite her qualifications, Noether experienced much resistance during her career. From one thousand nine hundred eight to one thousand nine hundred fifteen, she taught at Erlangen without pay. During this time, she caught the attention of David Hilbert, one of the world's foremost mathematicians of the time, who invited her to Goettingen. However, women were prohibited from obtaining professorships, and she was only able to lecture under Hilbert's name, again without pay. During this time she proved what is now known as Noether's theorem, which is still used in theoretical physics today. Noether was finally granted the right to teach in one thousand nine hundred nineteen. Hilbert's response to continued resistance of his university colleagues reportedly was: “Gentlemen, the faculty senate is not a bathhouse.” In the later nineteen twenties, she concentrated on work in abstract algebra, and her contributions revolutionized the field. In her proofs she often made use of the so-called ascending chain condition, which states that there is no infinite strictly increasing chain of certain sets. For instance, certain algebraic structures now known as Noetherian rings have the property that there are no infinite sequences of ideals I sub one is properly contained in I sub two, which is properly contained in the next ideal, and so on . The condition can be generalized to any partial order (in algebra, it concerns the special case of ideals ordered by the subset relation), and we can also consider the dual descending chain condition, where every strictly de creasing sequence in a partial order eventually ends. If a partial order satisfies the descending chain condition, it is possible to use induction along this order in a similar way in which we can use induction along the less than order on the natural numbers . Such orders are called well-founded or Noetherian, and the corresponding proof principle Noetherian induction. Noether was Jewish, and when the Nazis came to power in one thousand nine hundred thirty three, she was dismissed from her position. Luckily, Noether was able to emigrate to the United States for a temporary position at Bryn Mawr, Pennsylvania. During her time there she also lectured at Princeton, although she found the university to be unwelcoming to women (Auguste Dick, one thousand nine hundred eighty one, eighty one) . In one thousand nine hundred thirty five, Noether underwent an operation to remove a uterine tumour. She died from an infection as a result of the surgery, and was buried at Bryn Mawr. Further reading. For a biography of Noether, see Auguste Dick (one thousand nine hundred eighty one) . The Perimeter Institute for Theoretical Physics has their lectures on Noether's life and influence available online (Perimeter Institute, two thousand fifteen) . If you're tired of reading, Stuff You Missed in History Class has a podcast on Noether's life and influence (Holly Frey and Tracy V. Wilson, two thousand fifteen) . The collected works of Noether are available in the original German (Nathan Jacobson, one thousand nine hundred eighty three) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Rozsa Peter. Rozsa Peter was born Rosza Politzer, in Budapest, Hungary, on February seventeen, one thousand nine hundred five. She is best known for her work on recursive functions, which was essential for the creation of the field of recursion theory. Peter was raised during harsh political times—WWI raged when she was a teenager—but was able to attend the affluent Maria Terezia Girls' School in Budapest, from where she graduated in one thousand nine hundred twenty two. She then studied at Pazmany Peter University (later renamed Lorand Eoetvoes University) in Budapest. She began studying chemistry at the insistence of her father, but later switched to mathematics, and graduated in one thousand nine hundred twenty seven. Although she had the credentials to teach high school mathematics, the economic situation at the time was dire as the Great Depression affected the world economy. During this time, Peter took odd jobs as a tutor and private teacher of mathematics. She eventually returned to university to take up graduate studies in mathematics. She had originally planned to work in number theory, but after finding out that her results had already been proven, she almost gave up on mathematics altogether. She was encouraged to work on Goedel's incompleteness theorems, and unknowingly proved several of his results in different ways. This restored her confidence, and Peter went on to write her first papers on recursion theory, inspired by David Hilbert's foundational program. She received her PhD in one thousand nine hundred thirty five, and in one thousand nine hundred thirty seven she became an editor for the Journal of Symbolic Logic. Peter's early papers are widely credited as founding contributions to the field of recursive function theory. In Rózsa Péter (one thousand nine hundred thirty fivea) , she investigated the relationship between different kinds of recursion. In Rózsa Péter (one thousand nine hundred thirty fiveb) , she showed that a certain recursively defined function is not primitive recursive. This simplified an earlier result due to Wilhelm Ackermann. Peter's simplified function is what's now often called the Ackermann function—and sometimes, more properly, the Ackermann—Peter function. She wrote the first book on recursive function theory (Rózsa Péter, one thousand nine hundred fifty one) . Despite the importance and influence of her work, Peter did not obtain a full-time teaching position until one thousand nine hundred forty five. During the Nazi occupation of Hungary during World War II, Peter was not allowed to teach due to anti-Semitic laws. In one thousand nine hundred forty four the government created a Jewish ghetto in Budapest; the ghetto was cut off from the rest of the city and attended by armed guards. Peter was forced to live in the ghetto until one thousand nine hundred forty five when it was liberated. She then went on to teach at the Budapest Teachers Training College, and from one thousand nine hundred fifty five onward at Eoetvoes Lorand University. She was the first female Hungarian mathematician to become an Academic Doctor of Mathematics, and the first woman to be elected to the Hungarian Academy of Sciences. Peter was known as a passionate teacher of mathematics, who preferred to explore the nature and beauty of mathematical problems with her students rather than to merely lecture. As a result, she was affectionately called “Aunt Rosa” by her students. Peter died in one thousand nine hundred seventy seven at the age of seventy one. Further reading. For more biographical reading, see (John J. O'Connor and Edmund F. Robertson, two thousand fourteen) and (Béla Andrásfai, one thousand nine hundred eighty six) . Istvan Tamassy (one thousand nine hundred ninety four) conducted a brief interview with Peter. For a fun read about mathematics, see Peter's book Playing With Infinity (Rózsa Péter, two thousand ten) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Julia Robinson. Julia Bowman Robinson was an American mathematician. She is known mainly for her work on decision problems, and most famously for her contributions to the solution of Hilbert's tenth problem. Robinson was born in St. Louis, Missouri, on December eight, one thousand nine hundred nineteen. Robinson recalls being intrigued by numbers already as a child (Constance Reid, one thousand nine hundred eighty six, four) . At age nine she contracted scarlet fever and suffered from several recurrent bouts of rheumatic fever. This forced her to spend much of her time in bed, putting her behind in her education. Although she was able to catch up with the help of private tutors, the physical effects of her illness had a lasting impact on her life. Despite her childhood struggles, Robinson graduated high school with several awards in mathematics and the sciences. She started her university career at San Diego State College, and transferred to the University of California, Berkeley, as a senior. There she was influenced by the mathematician Raphael Robinson. They became good friends, and married in one thousand nine hundred forty one. As a spouse of a faculty member, Robinson was barred from teaching in the mathematics department at Berkeley. Although she continued to audit mathematics classes, she hoped to leave university and start a family. Not long after her wedding, however, Robinson contracted pneumonia. She was told that there was substantial scar tissue build up on her heart due to the rheumatic fever she suffered as a child. Due to the severity of the scar tissue, the doctor predicted that she would not live past forty and she was advised not to have children (Constance Reid, one thousand nine hundred eighty six, thirteen) . Robinson was depressed for a long time, but eventually decided to continue studying mathematics. She returned to Berkeley and completed her PhD in one thousand nine hundred forty eight under the supervision of Alfred Tarski. The first-order theory of the real numbers had been shown to be decidable by Tarski, and from Goedel's work it followed that the first-order theory of the natural numbers is undecidable. It was a major open problem whether the first-order theory of the rationals is decidable or not. In her thesis (one thousand nine hundred forty nine) , Robinson proved that it was not. Interested in decision problems, Robinson next attempted to find a solution to Hilbert's tenth problem. This problem was one of a famous list of twenty three mathematical problems posed by David Hilbert in one thousand nine hundred. The tenth problem asks whether there is an algorithm that will answer, in a finite amount of time, whether or not a polynomial equation with integer coefficients, such as three x squared minus two y plus three equals zero , has a solution in the integers. Such questions are known as Diophantine problems. After some initial successes, Robinson joined forces with Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam, who were also working on the problem. They succeeded in showing that exponential Diophantine problems (where the unknowns may also appear as exponents) are undecidable, and showed that a certain conjecture (later called “J.R.”) implies that Hilbert's tenth problem is undecidable (Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam and Julia Robinson, one thousand nine hundred sixty one) . Robinson continued to work on the problem throughout the nineteen sixties. In one thousand nine hundred seventy, the young Russian mathematician Yuri Matijasevich finally proved the J.R. hypothesis. The combined result is now called the Matijasevich—Robinson—Davis—Putnam theorem, or MRDP theorem for short. Matijasevich and Robinson became friends and collaborated on several papers. In a letter to Matijasevich, Robinson once wrote that “actually I am very pleased that working together (thousands of miles apart) we are obviously making more progress than either one of us could alone” (Yuri Matijasevich, one thousand nine hundred ninety two, forty five) . Robinson was the first female president of the American Mathematical Society, and the first woman to be elected to the National Academy of Science. She died on July thirty, one thousand nine hundred eighty five at the age of sixty five after being diagnosed with leukemia. Further reading. Robinson's mathematical papers are available in her Collected Works (Julia Robinson, one thousand nine hundred ninety six) , which also includes a reprint of her National Academy of Sciences biographical memoir (Solomon Feferman, one thousand nine hundred ninety four) . Robinson's older sister Constance Reid published an “Autobiography of Julia,” based on interviews (Constance Reid, one thousand nine hundred eighty six) , as well as a full memoir (Constance Reid, one thousand nine hundred ninety six) . A short documentary about Robinson and Hilbert's tenth problem was directed by George Csicsery (George Csicsery, two thousand sixteen) . For a brief memoir about Yuri Matijasevich's collaborations with Robinson, and her influence on his work, see (Yuri Matijasevich, one thousand nine hundred ninety two) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Bertrand Russell. Bertrand Russell is hailed as one of the founders of modern analytic philosophy. Born May eighteen, one thousand eight hundred seventy two, Russell was not only known for his work in philosophy and logic, but wrote many popular books in various subject areas. He was also an ardent political activist throughout his life. Russell was born in Trellech, Monmouthshire, Wales. His parents were members of the British nobility. They were free-thinkers, and even made friends with the radicals in Boston at the time. Unfortunately, Russell's parents died when he was young, and Russell was sent to live with his grandparents. There, he was given a religious upbringing (something his parents had wanted to avoid at all costs). His grandmother was very strict in all matters of morality. During adolescence he was mostly homeschooled by private tutors. Russell's influence in analytic philosophy, and especially logic, is tremendous. He studied mathematics and philosophy at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he was influenced by the mathematician and philosopher Alfred North Whitehead. In one thousand nine hundred ten, Russell and Whitehead published the first volume of Principia Mathematica, where they championed the view that mathematics is reducible to logic. He went on to publish hundreds of books, essays and political pamphlets. In one thousand nine hundred fifty, he won the Nobel Prize for literature. Russell's was deeply entrenched in politics and social activism. During World War I he was arrested and sent to prison for six months due to pacifist activities and protest. While in prison, he was able to write and read, and claims to have found the experience “quite agreeable.” He remained a pacifist throughout his life, and was again incarcerated for attending a nuclear disarmament rally in one thousand nine hundred sixty one. He also survived a plane crash in one thousand nine hundred forty eight, where the only survivors were those sitting in the smoking section. As such, Russell claimed that he owed his life to smoking. Russell was married four times, but had a reputation for carrying on extra-marital affairs. He died on February two, one thousand nine hundred seventy at the age of ninety seven in Penrhyndeudraeth, Wales. Further reading. Russell wrote an autobiography in three parts, spanning his life from one thousand eight hundred seventy two to one thousand nine hundred sixty seven (Bertrand Russell, one thousand nine hundred sixty seven) and (Bertrand Russell, one thousand nine hundred sixty eight) and (Bertrand Russell, one thousand nine hundred sixty nine) . The Bertrand Russell Research Centre at McMaster University is home of the Bertrand Russell archives. See their website at Arlene Duncan (two thousand fifteen) , for information on the volumes of his collected works (including searchable indexes), and archival projects. Russell's paper On Denoting (Bertrand Russell, one thousand nine hundred five) is a classic of twentyth century analytic philosophy. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Russell (Andrew David Irvine, two thousand fifteen) has sound clips of Russell speaking on Desire and Political theory. Many video interviews with Russell are available online. To see him talk about smoking and being involved in a plane crash, for example, see Bertrand Russell (n.d.) . Some of Russell's works, including his Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy are available as free audiobooks on LibriVox (n.d.) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Alfred Tarski. Alfred Tarski was born on January fourteen, one thousand nine hundred one in Warsaw, Poland (then part of the Russian Empire). Described as “Napoleonic,” Tarski was boisterous, talkative, and intense. His energy was often reflected in his lectures—he once set fire to a wastebasket while disposing of a cigarette during a lecture, and was forbidden from lecturing in that building again. Tarski had a thirst for knowledge from a young age. Although later in life he would tell students that he studied logic because it was the only class in which he got a B, his high school records show that he got A's across the board—even in logic. He studied at the University of Warsaw from one thousand nine hundred eighteen to one thousand nine hundred twenty four. Tarski first intended to study biology, but became interested in mathematics, philosophy, and logic, as the university was the center of the Warsaw School of Logic and Philosophy. Tarski earned his doctorate in one thousand nine hundred twenty four under the supervision of Stanisław Lesniewski. Before emigrating to the United States in one thousand nine hundred thirty nine, Tarski completed some of his most important work while working as a secondary school teacher in Warsaw. His work on logical consequence and logical truth were written during this time. In one thousand nine hundred thirty nine, Tarski was visiting the United States for a lecture tour. During his visit, Germany invaded Poland, and because of his Jewish heritage, Tarski could not return. His wife and children remained in Poland until the end of the war, but were then able to emigrate to the United States as well. Tarski taught at Harvard, the College of the City of New York, and the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, and finally the University of California, Berkeley. There he founded the multidisciplinary program in Logic and the Methodology of Science. Tarski died on October twenty six, one thousand nine hundred eighty three at the age of eighty two. Further reading. For more on Tarski's life, see the biography Alfred Tarski: Life and Logic (Anita Feferman and Solomon Feferman, two thousand four) . Tarski's seminal works on logical consequence and truth are available in English in (John Corcoran, one thousand nine hundred eighty three) . All of Tarski's original works have been collected into a four volume series, (Alfred Tarski, one thousand nine hundred eighty one) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Alan Turing. Alan Turing was born in Maida Vale, London, on June twenty three, one thousand nine hundred twelve. He is considered the father of theoretical computer science. Turing's interest in the physical sciences and mathematics started at a young age. However, as a boy his interests were not represented well in his schools, where emphasis was placed on literature and classics. Consequently, he did poorly in school and was reprimanded by many of his teachers. Turing attended King's College, Cambridge as an undergraduate, where he studied mathematics. In one thousand nine hundred thirty six Turing developed (what is now called) the Turing machine as an attempt to precisely define the notion of a computable function and to prove the undecidability of the decision problem. He was beaten to the result by Alonzo Church, who proved the result via his own lambda calculus. Turing's paper was still published with reference to Church's result. Church invited Turing to Princeton, where he spent one thousand nine hundred thirty six to one thousand nine hundred thirty eight, and obtained a doctorate under Church. Despite his interest in logic, Turing's earlier interests in physical sciences remained prevalent. His practical skills were put to work during his service with the British cryptanalytic department at Bletchley Park during World War II. Turing was a central figure in cracking the cypher used by German Naval communications—the Enigma code. Turing's expertise in statistics and cryptography, together with the introduction of electronic machinery, gave the team the ability to crack the code by creating a de-crypting machine called a “bombe.” His ideas also helped in the creation of the world's first programmable electronic computer, the Colossus, also used at Bletchley park to break the German Lorenz cypher. Turing was gay. Nevertheless, in one thousand nine hundred forty two he proposed to Joan Clarke, one of his teammates at Bletchley Park, but later broke off the engagement and confessed to her that he was homosexual. He had several lovers throughout his lifetime, although homosexual acts were then criminal offences in the UK. In one thousand nine hundred fifty two, Turing's house was burgled by a friend of his lover at the time, and when filing a police report, Turing admitted to having a homosexual relationship, under the impression that the government was on their way to legalizing homosexual acts. This was not true, and he was charged with gross indecency. Instead of going to prison, Turing opted for a hormone treatment that reduced libido. Turing was found dead on June eight, one thousand nine hundred fifty four, of a cyanide overdose—most likely suicide. He was given a royal pardon by Queen Elizabeth II in two thousand thirteen. Further reading. For a comprehensive biography of Alan Turing, see Andrew Hodges (two thousand fourteen) . Turing's life and work inspired a play, Breaking the Code, which was produced in one thousand nine hundred ninety six for TV starring Derek Jacobi as Turing. The Imitation Game, an Academy Award nominated film starring Bendict Cumberbatch and Kiera Knightley, is also loosely based on Alan Turing's life and time at Bletchley Park (Morten Tyldum, two thousand fourteen) . Radiolab (two thousand twelve) has several podcasts on Turing's life and work. BBC Horizon's documentary The Strange Life and Death of Dr. Turing is available to watch online (Christopher Sykes, one thousand nine hundred ninety two) . (Andre Theelen, two thousand twelve) is a short video of a working LEGO Turing Machine—made to honour Turing's centenary in two thousand twelve. Turing's original paper on Turing machines and the decision problem is Alan M. Turing (one thousand nine hundred thirty seven) . End further reading. End section.

Section. Ernst Zermelo. Ernst Zermelo was born on July twenty seven, one thousand eight hundred seventy one in Berlin, Germany. He had five sisters, though his family suffered from poor health and only three survived to adulthood. His parents also passed away when he was young, leaving him and his siblings orphans when he was seventeen. Zermelo had a deep interest in the arts, and especially in poetry. He was known for being sharp, witty, and critical. His most celebrated mathematical achievements include the introduction of the axiom of choice (in one thousand nine hundred four), and his axiomatization of set theory (in one thousand nine hundred eight). Zermelo's interests at university were varied. He took courses in physics, mathematics, and philosophy. Under the supervision of Hermann Schwarz, Zermelo completed his dissertation Investigations in the Calculus of Variations in one thousand eight hundred ninety four at the University of Berlin. In one thousand eight hundred ninety seven, he decided to pursue more studies at the University of Goettigen, where he was heavily influenced by the foundational work of David Hilbert. In one thousand eight hundred ninety nine he became eligible for professorship, but did not get one until eleven years later—possibly due to his strange demeanour and “nervous haste.” Zermelo finally received a paid professorship at the University of Zurich in one thousand nine hundred ten, but was forced to retire in one thousand nine hundred sixteen due to tuberculosis. After his recovery, he was given an honourary professorship at the University of Freiburg in one thousand nine hundred twenty one. During this time he worked on foundational mathematics. He became irritated with the works of Thoralf Skolem and Kurt Goedel, and publicly criticized their approaches in his papers. He was dismissed from his position at Freiburg in one thousand nine hundred thirty five, due to his unpopularity and his opposition to Hitler's rise to power in Germany. The later years of Zermelo's life were marked by isolation. After his dismissal in one thousand nine hundred thirty five, he abandoned mathematics. He moved to the country where he lived modestly. He married in one thousand nine hundred forty four, and became completely dependent on his wife as he was going blind. Zermelo lost his sight completely by one thousand nine hundred fifty one. He passed away in Guenterstal, Germany, on May twenty one, one thousand nine hundred fifty three. Further reading.
