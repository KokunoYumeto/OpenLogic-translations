1% Part: incompleteness2% Chapter: incompleteness-provability3% Section: introduction45\documentclass[../../../include/open-logic-section]{subfiles}67\begin{document}89\olfileid{inc}{inp}{int}1011\olsection{Introduction}1213Hilbert thought that a system of axioms for a mathematical structure,14such as the natural numbers, is inadequate unless it allows one to15derive all true statements about the structure. Combined with his16later interest in formal systems of deduction, this suggests that he17thought that we should guarantee that, say, the formal systems we are18using to reason about the natural numbers is not only consistent, but19also \emph{complete}, i.e., every statement in its language is either20!!{derivable} or its negation is. G\"odel's first incompleteness theorem21shows that no such system of axioms exists: there is no complete,22consistent, !!{axiomatizable} formal system for arithmetic.  In fact,23no ``sufficiently strong,'' consistent, !!{axiomatizable} mathematical24theory is complete.2526A more important goal of Hilbert's, the centerpiece of his program for27the justification of modern (``classical'') mathematics, was to find28finitary consistency proofs for formal systems representing classical29reasoning.  With regard to Hilbert's program, then, G\"odel's second30incompleteness theorem was a much bigger blow. The second31incompleteness theorem can be stated in vague terms, like the first32incompleteness theorem. Roughly speaking, it says that no sufficiently33strong theory of arithmetic can prove its own consistency. We will34have to take ``sufficiently strong'' to include a little bit more35than~$\Th{Q}$.3637The idea behind G\"odel's original proof of the incompleteness theorem38can be found in the Epimenides paradox. Epimenides, a Cretan, asserted39that all Cretans are liars; a more direct form of the paradox is the40assertion ``this sentence is false.'' Essentially, by replacing truth41with !!{derivability}, G\"odel was able to formalize !!a{sentence}42which, in a roundabout way, asserts that it itself is not43!!{derivable}.  If that !!{sentence} were !!{derivable}, the theory44would then be inconsistent.  G\"odel showed that the negation of that45!!{sentence} is also not !!{derivable} from the system of axioms he was46considering. (For this second part, G\"odel had to assume that the47theory~$\Th{T}$ is what's called ``$\omega$-consistent.''48$\omega$-Consistency is related to consistency, but is a stronger49property.\footnote{That is, any $\omega$-consistent theory is50consistent, but not vice versa.} A few years after G\"odel, Rosser51showed that assuming simple consistency of~$\Th{T}$ is enough.)5253The first challenge is to understand how one can construct !!a{sentence}54that refers to itself. For every !!{formula}~$!A$ in the language55of~$\Th{Q}$, let~$\gn{!A}$ denote the numeral corresponding56to~$\Gn{!A}$. Think about what this means: $!A$~is !!a{formula} in the57language of~$\Th{Q}$, $\Gn{!A}$~is a natural number, and $\gn{!A}$~is58a \emph{term} in the language of~$\Th{Q}$. So every !!{formula}~$!A$59in the language of~$\Th{Q}$ has a \emph{name},~$\gn{!A}$, which is a60term in the language of~$\Th{Q}$; this provides us with a conceptual61framework in which !!{formula}s in the language of~$\Th{Q}$ can ``say''62things about other !!{formula}s. The following lemma is known as the63fixed-point lemma.6465\begin{lem}66Let $\Th{T}$ be any theory extending~$\Th{Q}$, and let $!B(x)$ be any67!!{formula} with only the variable~$x$ free. Then there is68!!a{sentence}~$!A$ such that $\Th{T} \Proves!A \liff !B(\gn{!A})$.69\end{lem}7071The lemma asserts that given any property $!B(x)$, there is72!!a{sentence}~$!A$ that asserts ``$!B(x)$ is true of me,'' and73$\Th{T}$ ``knows'' this.7475How can we construct such !!a{sentence}? Consider the following version76of the Epimenides paradox, due to Quine:77\begin{quote}78``Yields falsehood when preceded by its quotation'' yields falsehood79when preceded by its quotation.80\end{quote}81This sentence is not directly self-referential. It simply makes an82assertion about the syntactic objects between quotes, and, in doing83so, it is on par with sentences like84\begin{enumerate}85\item ``Robert'' is a nice name.86\item ``I ran.'' is a short sentence.87\item ``Has three words'' has three words.88\end{enumerate}89But what happens when one takes the phrase ``yields falsehood when90preceded by its quotation,'' and precedes it with a quoted version of91itself? Then one has the original sentence!{} In short, the sentence92asserts that it is false.9394\end{document}

1% Part: incompleteness2% Chapter: incompleteness-provability3% Section: fixed-point-lemma45\documentclass[../../../include/open-logic-section]{subfiles}67\begin{document}89\olfileid{inc}{inp}{fix}1011\olsection{The Fixed-Point Lemma}1213\begin{explain}14The fixed-point lemma says that for any !!{formula}~$!B(x)$, there is15!!a{sentence}~$!A$ such that $\Th{T} \Proves !A \liff !B(\gn{!A})$,16provided $\Th{T}$ extends~$\Th{Q}$.  In the case of the liar sentence,17we'd want $!A$ to be equivalent (provably in~$\Th{T}$) to~``$\gn{!A}$18is false,'' i.e., the statement that $\Gn{!A}$ is the G\"odel number19of a false !!{sentence}. To understand the idea of the proof, it will20be useful to compare it with Quine's informal gloss of~$!A$ as,21``{}`yields a falsehood when preceded by its own quotation' yields a22falsehood when preceded by its own quotation.''  The operation of23taking an expression, and then forming a sentence by preceding this24expression by its own quotation may be called \emph{diagonalizing} the25expression, and the result its diagonalization. So, the26diagonalization of `yields a falsehood when preceded by its own27quotation' is ``{}`yields a falsehood when preceded by its own28quotation' yields a falsehood when preceded by its own quotation.''29Now note that Quine's liar sentence is not the diagonalization of30`yields a falsehood' but of `yields a falsehood when preceded by its31own quotation.' So the property being diagonalized to yield the liar32sentence itself involves diagonalization!{}3334In the language of arithmetic, we form quotations of !!a{formula} with35one free variable by computing its G\"odel numbers and then36substituting the standard numeral for that G\"odel number into the37free variable. The diagonalization of~$!E(x)$ is $!E(\num{n})$, where38$n = \Gn{!E(x)}$. (From now on, let's abbreviate $\num{\Gn{!E(x)}}$ as39$\gn{!E(x)}$.)  So if $!B(x)$ is ``is a falsehood,'' then ``yields a40falsehood if preceded by its own quotation,'' would be ``yields a41falsehood when applied to the G\"odel number of its diagonalization.''42If we had a symbol~$\Obj{diag}$ for the function $\fn{diag}(n)$ which43computes the G\"odel number of the diagonalization of the !!{formula}44with G\"odel number~$n$, we could write $!E(x)$ as45$!B(\Obj{diag}(x))$. And Quine's version of the liar sentence would46then be the diagonalization of it, i.e., $!E(\gn{!E(x)})$ or47$!B(\Obj{diag}(\gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}))$.  Of course, $!B(x)$ could48now be any other property, and the same construction would work. For49the incompleteness theorem, we'll take $!B(x)$ to be ``$x$~is not50!!{derivable} in~$\Th{T}$.'' Then $!E(x)$ would be ``yields51!!a{sentence} not !!{derivable} in~$\Th{T}$ when applied to the52G\"odel number of its diagonalization.''5354To formalize this in~$\Th{T}$, we have to find a way to formalize55$\fn{diag}$. The function $\fn{diag}(n)$ is computable, in fact, it is56primitive recursive: if $n$ is the G\"odel number of a57formula~$!E(x)$, $\fn{diag}(n)$ returns the G\"odel number58of~$!E(\gn{!E(x)})$. (Recall, $\gn{!E(x)}$ is the standard numeral of59the G\"odel number of~$!E(x)$, i.e., $\num{\Gn{!E(x)}}$). If60$\Obj{diag}$ were a function symbol in $\Th{T}$ representing the61function $\fn{diag}$, we could take $!A$ to be the formula62$!B(\Obj{diag}(\gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}))$. Notice that63\begin{align*}64\fn{diag}(\Gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}) & = 65\Gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(\gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}))} \\66& = \Gn{!A}.67\end{align*}68Assuming $\Th{T}$ can !!{derive}69\[70\Obj{diag}(\gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}) = \gn{!A},71\]72it can !!{derive} $!B(\Obj{diag}(\gn{!B(\Obj{diag}(x))}))73\liff !B(\gn{!A})$. But the left hand side is, by74definition,~$!A$.7576Of course, $\Obj{diag}$ will in general not be a function symbol of77$\Th{T}$, and certainly is not one of~$\Th{Q}$. But, since $\fn{diag}$78is computable, it is \emph{representable} in~$\Th{Q}$ by some formula79$!D_{\fn{diag}}(x,y)$. So instead of writing $!B(\Obj{diag}(x))$ we80can write $\lexists[y][(!D_{\fn{diag}}(x,y) \land !B(y))]$. Otherwise,81the proof sketched above goes through, and in fact, it goes through82already in~$\Th{Q}$.83\end{explain}8485\begin{lem}86\ollabel{lem:fixed-point} Let $!B(x)$ be any formula with one free87variable~$x$. Then there is a sentence~$!A$ such that $\Th{Q} \Proves88!A \liff !B(\gn{!A})$.89\end{lem}909192\begin{proof}93Given $!B(x)$, let $!E(x)$ be the formula94$\lexists[y][(!D_{\fn{diag}}(x,y) \land !B(y))]$ and let $!A$~be its95diagonalization, i.e., the formula $!E(\gn{!E(x)})$.9697Since $!D_{\fn{diag}}$ represents $\fn{diag}$, and98$\fn{diag}(\Gn{!E(x)}) = \Gn{!A}$, $\Th{Q}$ can !!{derive}99\begin{align}100  & !D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)}, \gn{!A}) \ollabel{repdiag1} \\101  & \lforall[y][(!D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)},y) \lif102  \eq[y][\gn{!A}])]. \ollabel{repdiag2}103\end{align}104Now we show that $\Th{Q} \Proves !A \liff !B(\gn{!A})$. We argue105informally, using just logic and facts !!{derivable} in~$\Th{Q}$.106107First, suppose~$!A$, i.e., $!E(\gn{!E(x)})$. Going back to the108definition of $!E(x)$, we see that $!E(\gn{!E(x)})$ just is109\[110\lexists[y][(!D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)},y) \land !B(y))].111\]112Consider such a~$y$. Since $!D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)},y)$, by113\olref{repdiag2}, $y = \gn{!A}$. So, from $!B(y)$ we114have~$!B(\gn{!A})$.115116Now suppose $!B(\gn{!A})$. By \olref{repdiag1}, we have117\begin{align*}118& !D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)}, \gn{!A}) \land !B(\gn{!A}).119\intertext{It follows120that}121& \lexists[y][(!D_{\fn{diag}}(\gn{!E(x)},y) \land !B(y))].122\end{align*}123But that's just $!E(\gn{!E(x)})$, i.e.,~$!A$.124\end{proof}125126\begin{digress}127You should compare this to the proof of the fixed-point lemma in128computability theory. The difference is that here we want to define a129\emph{statement} in terms of itself, whereas there we wanted to define130a \emph{function} in terms of itself; this difference aside, it is131really the same idea.132\end{digress}133134\begin{prob} 135  !!^a{formula}~$!A(x)$ is a \emph{truth definition} if $\Th{Q}136  \Proves !B \liff !A(\gn{!B})$ for all !!{sentence}s~$!B$. Show that137  no !!{formula} is a truth definition by using the fixed-point lemma.138\end{prob}139140\end{document}

1% Part: incompleteness2% Chapter: incompleteness-provability3% Section: first-incompleteness-thm45\documentclass[../../../include/open-logic-section]{subfiles}67\begin{document}89\olfileid{inc}{inp}{1in}1011\olsection{The First Incompleteness Theorem}1213We can now describe G\"odel's original proof of the first14incompleteness theorem. Let $\Th{T}$ be any computably axiomatized theory15in a language extending the language of arithmetic, such that $\Th{T}$16includes the axioms of $\Th{Q}$. This means that, in particular, $\Th{T}$17represents computable functions and relations.1819We have argued that, given a reasonable coding of formulas and proofs20as numbers, the relation $\Prf[\Th{T}](x,y)$ is computable, where21$\Prf[\Th{T}](x,y)$ holds if and only if $x$ is the G\"odel number of22!!a{derivation} of the !!{formula} with G\"odel number~$y$23in~$\Th{T}$. In fact, for the particular theory that G\"odel had in24mind, G\"odel was able to show that this relation is primitive25recursive, using the list of 45 functions and relations in his26paper. The 45th relation, $x B y$, is just $\Prf[\Th{T}](x,y)$ for his27particular choice of~$\Th{T}$. Remember that where G\"odel uses the28word ``recursive'' in his paper, we would now use the phrase29``primitive recursive.''3031Since $\Prf[\Th{T}](x,y)$ is computable, it is representable in $\Th{T}$. We32will use $\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,y)$ to refer to the formula that represents33it. Let $\OProv[\Th{T}](y)$ be the formula34$\lexists[x][\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,y)]$. This describes the 46th relation,35$\fn{Bew}(y)$, on G\"odel's list. As G\"odel notes, this is the only36relation that ``cannot be asserted to be recursive.''  What he37probably meant is this: from the definition, it is not clear that it38is computable; and later developments, in fact, show that it isn't.3940Let $\Th{T}$ be an !!{axiomatizable} theory containing~$\Th{Q}$. Then41$\Prf[\Th{T}](x, y)$ is decidable, hence representable in~$\Th{Q}$ by42!!a{formula}~$\OPrf[\Th{T}](x, y)$. Let $\OProv[\Th{T}](y)$ be the formula we43described above. By the fixed-point lemma, there is a formula44$!G_\Th{T}$ such that $\Th{Q}$ (and hence $\Th{T}$) !!{derive}s45\begin{equation}46\ollabel{eqn:qpf}47!G_\Th{T} \liff \lnot \OProv[\Th{T}](\gn{!G_\Th{T}}).48\end{equation}49Note that $!G_\Th{T}$ says, in essence, ``$!G_\Th{T}$ is not50!!{derivable} in~$\Th{T}$.''5152\begin{lem}\ollabel{lem:cons-G-unprov}53If $\Th{T}$ is a consistent, !!{axiomatizable} theory54extending~$\Th{Q}$, then $\Th{T} \Proves/ !G_\Th{T}$.55\end{lem}5657\begin{proof}58Suppose $\Th{T}$ !!{derive}s $!G_\Th{T}$. Then there \emph{is}59!!a{derivation}, and so, for some number $m$, the relation $\Prf[\Th{T}](m,60\Gn{!G_\Th{T}})$ holds. But then $\Th{Q}$ !!{derive}s the sentence61$\OPrf[\Th{T}](\num m, \gn{!G_\Th{T}})$. So $\Th{Q}$ !!{derive}s62$\lexists[x][\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,\gn{!G_\Th{T}})]$, which is, by definition,63$\OProv[\Th{T}](\gn{!G_\Th{T}})$. By \olref{eqn:qpf}, $\Th{Q}$ !!{derive}s64$\lnot !G_\Th{T}$, and since $\Th{T}$ extends $\Th{Q}$, so65does~$\Th{T}$. We have shown that if $\Th{T}$ !!{derive}s $!G_\Th{T}$, then66it also !!{derive}s $\lnot !G_\Th{T}$, and hence it would be inconsistent.67\end{proof}6869\begin{defn}70\ollabel{thm:oconsis-q}71A theory $\Th{T}$ is \emph{$\omega$-consistent} if the following holds: if72$\lexists[x][!A(x)]$ is any sentence and $\Th{T}$ !!{derive}s $\lnot73!A(\num 0)$, $\lnot !A(\num 1)$, $\lnot !A(\num 2)$, \dots then $\Th{T}$74does not prove $\lexists[x][!A(x)]$.75\end{defn}7677Note that every $\omega$-consistent theory is also consistent. This78follows simply from the fact that if $\Th{T}$ is inconsistent, then79$\Th{T} \Proves !A$ for every~$!A$. In particular, if $\Th{T}$ is80inconsistent, it !!{derive}s both $\lnot !A(\num n)$ for every~$n$ and81also !!{derive}s~$\lexists[x][!A(x)]$. So, if $\Th{T}$ is82inconsistent, it is $\omega$-inconsistent. By contraposition, if83$\Th{T}$ is $\omega$-consistent, it must be consistent.8485\begin{lem}\ollabel{lem:omega-cons-G-unref}86If $\Th{T}$ is an $\omega$-consistent, !!{axiomatizable} theory87extending~$\Th{Q}$, then $\Th{T} \Proves/ \lnot !G_\Th{T}$.88\end{lem}8990\begin{proof}91We show that if $\Th{T}$ !!{derive}s $\lnot !G_\Th{T}$, then it is92$\omega$-inconsistent. Suppose $\Th{T}$ !!{derive}s $\lnot !G_\Th{T}$. If93$\Th{T}$ is inconsistent, it is $\omega$-inconsistent, and we are94done. Otherwise, $\Th{T}$ is consistent, so it does not !!{derive}95$!G_\Th{T}$ by \olref{lem:cons-G-unprov}. Since there is no96!!{derivation} of $!G_\Th{T}$ in $\Th{T}$, $\Th{Q}$ !!{derive}s97\[98\lnot \OPrf[\Th{T}](\num 0, \gn{!G_\Th{T}}), \lnot \OPrf[\Th{T}](\num 1,99\gn{!G_\Th{T}}), \lnot \OPrf[\Th{T}](\num 2, \gn{!G_\Th{T}}), \dots100\]101and so does~$\Th{T}$.  On the other hand, by \olref{eqn:qpf}, $\lnot102!G_\Th{T}$ is equivalent to103$\lexists[x][\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,\gn{!G_\Th{T}})]$. So $\Th{T}$ is104$\omega$-inconsistent.105\end{proof}106107\begin{prob}108  Every $\omega$-consistent theory is consistent. Show that the109  converse does not hold, i.e., that there are consistent but110  $\omega$-inconsistent theories. Do this by showing that $\Th{Q} \cup111  \{\lnot !G_\Th{Q}\}$ is consistent but $\omega$-inconsistent.112\end{prob}113114\begin{thm}115\ollabel{thm:first-incompleteness} Let $\Th{T}$ be any116$\omega$-consistent, !!{axiomatizable} theory extending~$\Th{Q}$. Then117$\Th{T}$ is not complete.118\end{thm}119120\begin{proof}121  If $\Th{T}$ is $\omega$-consistent, it is consistent, so $\Th{T}122  \Proves/ !G_\Th{T}$ by \olref{lem:cons-G-unprov}.  By123  \olref{lem:omega-cons-G-unref}, $\Th{T} \Proves/ \lnot !G_\Th{T}$.124  This means that $\Th{T}$ is incomplete, since it !!{derive}s neither125  $!G_\Th{T}$ nor $\lnot !G_\Th{T}$.126\end{proof}127128\end{document}

1% Part: incompleteness2% Chapter: incompleteness-provability3% Section: rosser-thm45\documentclass[../../../include/open-logic-section]{subfiles}67\begin{document}89\olfileid{inc}{inp}{ros}1011\olsection{Rosser's Theorem}1213Can we modify G\"odel's proof to get a stronger result, replacing14``$\omega$-consistent'' with simply ``consistent''? The answer is15``yes,'' using a trick discovered by Rosser.  Rosser's trick is to use16a ``modified'' !!{derivability} predicate $\ORProv_T(y)$ instead of17$\OProv[\Th{T}](y)$.1819\begin{thm}20\ollabel{thm:rosser}21Let $\Th{T}$ be any consistent, !!{axiomatizable} theory22extending $\Th{Q}$. Then $\Th{T}$ is not complete.23\end{thm}2425\begin{proof}26Recall that $\OProv[\Th{T}](y)$ is defined as $\lexists[x][\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,27  y)]$, where $\OPrf[\Th{T}](x, y)$ represents the decidable relation which28holds iff $x$ is the G\"odel number of !!a{derivation} of the29!!{sentence} with G\"odel number~$y$. The relation that holds between30$x$ and~$y$ if $x$~is the G\"odel number of a \emph{refutation} of the31sentence with G\"odel number~$y$ is also decidable. Let $\fn{not}(x)$32be the primitive recursive function which does the following: if $x$33is the code of a formula $!A$, $\fn{not}(x)$ is a code of $\lnot34!A$. Then $\Refut[\Th{T}](x, y)$ holds iff $\Prf[\Th{T}](x, \fn{not}(y))$.  Let35$\ORefut[\Th{T}](x, y)$ represent it.  Then, if $\Th{T} \Proves \lnot !A$36and $\delta$ is a corresponding !!{derivation}, $\Th{Q} \Proves37\ORefut[\Th{T}](\gn{\delta}, \gn{!A})$.  We define $\ORProv[\Th{T}](y)$ as38\[39\lexists[x][(\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,y) \land \lforall[z][(z < x \lif \lnot40  \ORefut[\Th{T}](z,y))])].41\]42Roughly, $\ORProv[\Th{T}](y)$ says ``there is a proof of $y$ in $\Th{T}$,43and there is no shorter refutation of~$y$.''  Assuming $\Th{T}$ is44consistent, $\ORProv[\Th{T}](y)$ is true of the same numbers as45$\OProv[\Th{T}](y)$; but from the point of view of \emph{provability}46in~$\Th{T}$ (and we now know that there is a difference between truth47and provability!) the two have different properties. If $\Th{T}$ is48\emph{in}consistent, then the two do \emph{not} hold of the same49numbers!{} ($\ORProv[\Th{T}](y)$ is often read as ``$y$ is Rosser50provable.'' Since, as just discussed, Rosser provability is not some51special kind of provability---in inconsistent theories, there are52!!{sentence}s that are provable but not Rosser provable---this may be53confusing. To avoid the confusion, you could instead read it as54``$y$ is shmovable.'')5556By the fixed-point lemma, there is a formula $!R_\Th{T}$ such that57\begin{equation}58  \Th{Q} \Proves !R_\Th{T} \liff \lnot \ORProv[\Th{T}](\gn{!R_\Th{T}}).59  \ollabel{RT}60\end{equation}61In contrast to the proof of \olref[1in]{thm:first-incompleteness},62here we claim that if $\Th{T}$ is consistent, $\Th{T}$ doesn't !!{derive}63$!R_\Th{T}$, and $\Th{T}$ also doesn't !!{derive} $\lnot !R_\Th{T}$. (In64other words, we don't need the assumption of $\omega$-consistency.)6566First, let's show that $\Th{T} \Proves/ !R_{\Th{T}}$.  Suppose it did, so67there is !!a{derivation} of~$!R_{\Th{T}}$ from~$T$; let $n$ be its G\"odel68number. Then $\Th{Q} \Proves \OPrf[\Th{T}](\num{n}, \gn{!R_{\Th{T}}})$, since69$\OPrf[\Th{T}]$ represents $\Prf[\Th{T}]$ in~$\Th{Q}$. Also, for each $k < n$,70$k$ is not the G\"odel number of !!a{derivation} of $\lnot !R_{\Th{T}}$, since $\Th{T}$ is71consistent. So for each $k < n$, $\Th{Q} \Proves \lnot72\ORefut[\Th{T}](\num{k}, \gn{!R_{\Th{T}}})$. By \olref[req][min]{lem:less-nsucc},73$\Th{Q} \Proves \lforall[z][(z < \num{n} \lif \lnot \ORefut[\Th{T}](z,74  \gn{!R_{\Th{T}}}))]$. Thus,75\[76\Th{Q} \Proves \lexists[x][(\OPrf[\Th{T}](x,\gn{!R_{\Th{T}}}) \land \lforall[z][(z77    < x \lif \lnot \ORefut[\Th{T}](z,\gn{!R_{\Th{T}}}))])],78\]79but that's just $\ORProv[\Th{T}](\gn{!R_{\Th{T}}})$. By \olref{RT}, $\Th{Q}80\Proves \lnot !R_{\Th{T}}$. Since $\Th{T}$ extends $\Th{Q}$, also $\Th{T}81\Proves \lnot !R_{\Th{T}}$. We've assumed that $\Th{T} \Proves !R_{\Th{T}}$, so82$\Th{T}$ would be inconsistent, contrary to the assumption of the83theorem.8485Now, let's show that $\Th{T} \Proves/ \lnot !R_{\Th{T}}$. Again, suppose it86did, and suppose $n$ is the G\"odel number of !!a{derivation}87of~$\lnot !R_{\Th{T}}$.
