A theory is a set of sentences that is deductively closed, that is, with the property that whenever T proves A then A is in T . It is probably best to think of a theory as being a collection of sentences, together with all the things that these sentences imply. From now on, we will use Q to refer to the theory consisting of the set of sentences derivable from the eight axioms in the introduction to representability in Q and its eight axioms . Remember that we can code formula of Q as numbers; if A is such a formula, let the Goedel number of A denote the number coding A . Modulo this coding, we can now ask whether various sets of formulas are computable or not. End section.

Section. Q is Computably enumerable -Complete. Theorem. Q is computably enumerable but not decidable. In fact, it is a complete computably enumerable set. End theorem. Proof. It is not hard to see that Q is computably enumerable, since it is the set of (codes for) sentences y such that there is a proof x of y in Q : Displayed expression. Q equals the set of y such that there exists x with x the code of a proof in Q of the sentence with code y. End displayed expression. But we know that the proof coding relation for Q holds of x and y is computable (in fact, primitive recursive), and any set that can be written in the above form is c.e. Saying that it is a complete c.e. set is equivalent to saying that K is many one reducible to Q , where K equals the set of x such that machine A subscript x halts on input x . So let us show that K is reducible to Q . Since Kleene's predicate T of e, x, and s is primitive recursive, it is representable in Q , say, by A subscript T . Then for every x , we have Displayed expression. If x belongs to K, then there exists s such that Kleene's T relation holds of x, x, and s. This implies that there exists s such that Q proves A subscript T of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and the numeral for s. This in turn implies that Q proves: there exists s such that A subscript T holds of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and s. End displayed expression. Conversely, if Q proves that there exists s such that A subscript T holds of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and s , then, in fact, for some natural number n the formula A subscript T of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and the numeral for n must be true. Section. omega -Consistent Extensions of Q are Undecidable. The proof that Q is c.e.-complete relied on the fact that any sentence provable in Q is “true” of the natural numbers. The next definition and theorem strengthen this theorem, by pinpointing just those aspects of “truth” that were needed in the proof above. Don't dwell on this theorem too long, though, because we will soon strengthen it even further. We include it mainly for historical purposes: Goedel's original paper used the notion of omega -consistency, but his result was strengthened by replacing omega -consistency with ordinary consistency soon after. Definition. A theory T is omega -consistent if the following holds: if the formula there exists x such that A of x is any sentence and T proves not A of the numeral zero , not A of the numeral one , not A of the numeral two , and so on then T does not prove there exists x such that A of x . End definition. Theorem. Let T be any omega -consistent theory that includes Q . Then T is not decidable. End theorem. Proof. If T includes Q , then T represents the computable functions and relations. We need only modify the previous proof. As above, if x belongs to K , then T proves there exists s such that A subscript T holds of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and s . Conversely, suppose T proves there exists s such that A subscript T holds of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and s . Then x must be in K : otherwise, there is no halting computation of machine x on input x ; since A subscript T represents Kleene's T relation, T proves not A subscript T of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and the numeral zero , not A subscript T of the numeral for x, the numeral for x, and the numeral one , and so on, making T omega -inconsistent. End proof. End section.

Section. Consistent Extensions of Q are Undecidable. Remember that a theory is consistent if it does not prove both A and not A for any formula A . Since anything follows from a contradiction, an inconsistent theory is trivial: every sentence is provable. Clearly, if a theory if omega -consistent, then it is consistent. But being consistent is a weaker requirement (that is, there are theories that are consistent but not omega -consistent.). We can weaken the assumption in the preceding definition of omega consistency to simple consistency to obtain a stronger theorem. Lemma. There is no “universal computable relation.” That is, there is no binary computable relation R of x and y , with the following property: whenever S of y is a unary computable relation, there is some k such that for every y , S of y is true if and only if R of k and y is true. End lemma. Proof. Suppose R of x and y is a universal computable relation. Let S of y be the relation not R of y and y . Since S of y is computable, for some k , S of y is equivalent to R of k and y . But then we have that S of k is equivalent to both R of k and k and not R of k and k , which is a contradiction. End proof. Theorem. Let T be any consistent theory that includes Q . Then T is not decidable. End theorem. Proof. Suppose T is a consistent, decidable extension of Q . We will obtain a contradiction by using T to define a universal computable relation. Let R of x and y hold if and only if Quotation. x codes a formula D of u , and T proves D of the numeral for y . End quotation. Since we are assuming that T is decidable, R is computable. Let us show that R is universal. If S of y is any computable relation, then it is representable in Q (and hence T ) by a formula D subscript S of u . Then for every n , we have Displayed expression. If S holds of the numeral for n, then T proves D subscript S of the numeral for n. This implies R of the Goedel number of D subscript S of u, and n. End displayed expression. and Displayed expression. If S does not hold of the numeral for n, then T proves not D subscript S of the numeral for n. Since theory T is consistent, this implies that T does not prove D subscript S of the numeral for n. This implies that R does not hold of the Goedel number of D subscript S of u, and n. Section. Axiomatizable Theories. A theory T is said to be axiomatizable if it has a computable set of axioms A . (Saying that A is a set of axioms for T means T equals the set of sentences A that are provable from the axiom set named A .) Any “reasonable” axiomatization of the natural numbers will have this property. In particular, any theory with a finite set of axioms is axiomatizable. Lemma. Suppose T is axiomatizable. Then T is computably enumerable. End lemma. Proof. Suppose A is a computable set of axioms for T . To determine if sentence A belongs to theory T , just search for a derivation of A from the axioms. Put slightly differently, A is in T if and only if there is a finite list of axioms B subscript one , and so on, B subscript k in A and a derivation of if the conjunction of B subscript one through B subscript k holds, then A in first-order logic. But we already know that any set with a definition of the form “there exists and so on such that and so on ” is computably enumerable, provided the second “ and so on ” is computable. End proof. End section.

Section. Axiomatizable Complete Theories are Decidable. A theory is said to be complete if for every sentence A , either A or not A is provable. Lemma. Suppose a theory T is complete and axiomatizable. Then T is decidable. End lemma. Proof. Suppose T is complete and A is a computable set of axioms. If T is inconsistent, it is clearly computable. (Algorithm: “just say yes.”) So we can assume that T is also consistent. To decide whether or not a sentence A is in T , simultaneously search for a derivation of A from T and a derivation of not A . Since T is complete, you are bound to find one or the other; and since T is consistent, if you find a derivation of not A , there is no derivation of A . Put in different terms, we already know that T is computably enumerable; so by a theorem we proved before, it suffices to show that the complement of T is computably enumerable also. But a formula A is in the complement of T if and only if not A is in T ; so the complement of T is many one reducible to T . End proof. End section.

Section. Q has no Complete, Consistent, Axiomatizable Extensions. Theorem. There is no complete, consistent, axiomatizable extension of Q . End theorem. Proof. We already know that there is no consistent, decidable extension of Q . But if T is complete and axiomatized, then it is decidable. End proof. This theorems is not that far from Goedel's original one thousand nine hundred thirty one formulation of the First Incompleteness Theorem. Aside from the more modern terminology, the key differences are this: Goedel has “ omega -consistent” instead of “consistent”; and he could not say “axiomatizable” in full generality, since the formal notion of computability was not in place yet. (The formal models of computability were developed over the following decade, including by Goedel, and in large part to be able to characterize the kinds of theories that are susceptible to the Goedel phenomenon.) The theorem says you can't have it all, namely, completeness, consistency, and axiomatizability. If you give up any one of these, though, you can have the other two: Q is consistent and computably axiomatized, but not complete; the inconsistent theory is complete, and computably axiomatized (say, by the singleton set containing the formula zero does not equal zero ), but not consistent; and the set of true sentence of arithmetic is complete and consistent, but it is not computably axiomatized. End section.

Section. Theories Consistent with Q are Undecidable. The following theorem says that not only is Q undecidable, but, in fact, any theory that does not disagree with Q is undecidable. Theorem. Let T be any theory in the language of arithmetic that is consistent with Q (that is, the union of theories T and Q is consistent). Then T is undecidable. End theorem. Proof. Remember that Q has a finite set of axioms, Q subscript one , and so on, Q subscript eight . We can even replace these by a single axiom, E is the conjunction of Q subscript one through Q subscript eight . Suppose T is a decidable theory consistent with Q . Let Displayed expression. C equals the set of sentences A such that theory T proves the implication from E to A. End displayed expression. We show that C would be a computable separation of Q and Q bar , a contradiction. First, if A is in Q , then A is provable from the axioms of Q ; by the deduction theorem, there is a derivation of if E then A in first-order logic. So A is in C . On the other hand, if A is in Q bar , then there is a proof of if E then not A in first-order logic. If T also proves if E then A , then T proves not E , in which case the union of theories T and Q is inconsistent. But we are assuming the union of theories T and Q is consistent, so T does not prove if E then A , and so A is not in C . We've shown that if A is in Q , then it is in C , and if A is in Q bar , then it is in the complement of C . So C is a computable separation, which is the contradiction we were looking for. End proof. This theorem is very powerful. For example, it implies: Corollary. First-order logic for the language of arithmetic (that is, the set of sentences A such that sentence A is provable in first order logic ) is undecidable. End corollary. Proof. First-order logic is the set of consequences of the empty set , which is consistent with Q . End proof. End section.

Section. Theories in which Q is Interpretable are Undecidable. We can strengthen these results even more. Informally, an interpretation of a language L subscript one in another language L subscript two involves defining the universe, relation symbols, and function symbols of L subscript one with formulas in L subscript two . Though we won't take the time to do this, one can make this definition precise. Theorem. Suppose T is a theory in a language in which one can interpret the language of arithmetic, in such a way that T is consistent with the interpretation of Q . Then T is undecidable. If T proves the interpretation of the axioms of Q , then no consistent extension of T is decidable. End theorem. The proof is just a small modification of the proof of the last theorem; one could use a counterexample to get a separation of Q and Q bar . One can take Z F C , Zermelo—Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice, to be an axiomatic foundation that is powerful enough to carry out a good deal of ordinary mathematics. In Z F C one can define the natural numbers, and via this interpretation, the axioms of Q are true. So we have Corollary. There is no decidable extension of Z F C . End corollary. Original source remains available in Source view. End note. Corollary. There is no complete, consistent, computably axiomatizable extension of Z F C . End corollary. The language of Z F C has only a single binary relation, the membership relation symbol . (In fact, you don't even need equality.) So we have Corollary. First-order logic for any language with a binary relation symbol is undecidable. End corollary. This result extends to any language with two unary function symbols, since one can use these to simulate a binary relation symbol. The results just cited are tight: it turns out that first-order logic for a language with only unary relation symbols and at most one unary function symbol is decidable. One more bit of trivia. We know that the set of sentences in the language the zero constant , the successor symbol , the addition symbol , the multiplication symbol , the less than relation symbol true in the standard model is undecidable. In fact, one can define the less than relation symbol in terms of the other symbols, and then one can define the addition symbol in terms of the multiplication symbol and the successor symbol . So the set of true sentences in the language the zero constant , the successor symbol , the multiplication symbol is undecidable. On the other hand, Presburger has shown that the set of sentences in the language the zero constant , the successor symbol , the addition symbol true in the language of arithmetic is decidable. The procedure is computationally infeasible, however. End section. End chapter.
