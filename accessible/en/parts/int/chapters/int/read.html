Constructive Reasoning

In contrast to extensions of classical logic by modal operators or second-order quantifiers, intuitionistic logic is “non-classical” in that it restricts classical logic. Classical logic is non-constructive in various ways. Intuitionistic logic is intended to capture a more “constructive” kind of reasoning characteristic of a kind of constructive mathematics. The following examples may serve to illustrate some of the underlying motivations.

Suppose someone claimed that they had determined a natural number nnsource with the property that if nnsource is even, the Riemann hypothesis is true, and if nnsource is odd, the Riemann hypothesis is false. Great news! Whether the Riemann hypothesis is true or not is one of the big open questions of mathematics, and they seem to have reduced the problem to one of calculation, that is, to the determination of whether a specific number is even or not.

What is the magic value of nnsource? They describe it as follows: nnsource is the natural number that is equal to 22source if the Riemann hypothesis is true, and 33source otherwise.

Angrily, you demand your money back. From a classical point of view, the description above does in fact determine a unique value of nnsource; but what you really want is a value of nnsource that is given explicitly.

To take another, perhaps less contrived example, consider the following question. We know that it is possible to raise an irrational number to a rational power, and get a rational result. For example, 22=2\sqrt{2}^2 = 2source. What is less clear is whether or not it is possible to raise an irrational number to an irrational power, and get a rational result. The following theorem answers this in the affirmative:

Theorem on irrational powers

There are irrational numbers aasource and bbsource such that aba^bsource is rational.

Proof

Consider 22\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}source. If this is rational, we are done: we can let a=b=2a = b = \sqrt{2}source. Otherwise, it is irrational. Then we have

(22)2=22·2=22=2,(\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}})^{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}^2 = 2,source

which is rational. So, in this case, let aasource be 22\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}source, and let bbsource be 2\sqrt 2source.

Does this constitute a valid proof? Most mathematicians feel that it does. But again, there is something a little bit unsatisfying here: we have proved the existence of a pair of real numbers with a certain property, without being able to say which pair of numbers it is. It is possible to prove the same result, but in such a way that the pair aasource, bbsource is given in the proof: take a=3a = \sqrt{3}source and b=log34b = \log_3 4source. Then

ab=3log34=31/2·log34=(3log34)1/2=41/2=2,a^b = \sqrt{3}^{\log_3 4} = 3^{1/2 \cdot \log_3 4} = (3^{\log_3 4})^{1/2} = 4^{1/2}= 2,source

since 3log3x=x3^{\log_3 x} = xsource.

Intuitionistic logic is designed to capture a kind of reasoning where moves like the one in the first proof are disallowed. Proving the existence of an xxsource satisfying A(x)!A(x)source means that you have to give a specific xxsource, and a proof that it satisfies A!Asource, like in the second proof. Proving that A!Asource or B!Bsource holds requires that you can prove one or the other.

Formally speaking, intuitionistic logic is what you get if you restrict a derivation system for classical logic in a certain way. From the mathematical point of view, these are just formal deductive systems, but, as already noted, they are intended to capture a kind of mathematical reasoning. One can take this to be the kind of reasoning that is justified on a certain philosophical view of mathematics (such as Brouwer's intuitionism); one can take it to be a kind of mathematical reasoning which is more “concrete” and satisfying (along the lines of Bishop's constructivism); and one can argue about whether or not the formal description captures the informal motivation. But whatever philosophical positions we may hold, we can study intuitionistic logic as a formally presented logic; and for whatever reasons, many mathematical logicians find it interesting to do so.

Syntax of Intuitionistic Logic

The syntax of intuitionistic logic is the same as that for propositional logic. In classical propositional logic it is possible to define connectives by others, e.g., one can define A→B!A \lif !Bsource by ¬A∨B\lnot !A \lor !Bsource, or A∨B!A \lor !Bsource by ¬(¬A∧¬B)\lnot(\lnot !A \land \lnot !B)source. Thus, presentations of classical logic often introduce some connectives as abbreviations for these definitions. This is not so in intuitionistic logic, with two exceptions: ¬A\lnot !Asource can be---and often is---defined as an abbreviation for A→⊥!A \lif \lfalsesource. Then, of course, ⊥\lfalsesource must not itself be defined! Also, A↔B!A \liff !Bsource can be defined, as in classical logic, as (A→B)∧(B→A)(!A \lif !B) \land (!B \lif !A)source.

Formulas of propositional intuitionistic logic are built up from propositional variables and the propositional constant ⊥\lfalsesource using logical connectives. We have:

  1. A denumerable set At0\PVarsource of propositional variables p0\Obj p_0source, p1\Obj p_1source, dots

  2. The propositional constant for falsity ⊥\lfalsesource.

  3. The logical connectives: ∧\landsource (conjunction), ∨\lorsource (disjunction), →\lifsource (conditional)

  4. Punctuation marks: (, ), and the comma.

Inductive definition of intuitionistic propositional formulas

[Formula] The set Frm(L0)\Frm[L_0]source of formulas of propositional intuitionistic logic is defined inductively as follows:

  1. ⊥\lfalsesource is an atomic formula.

  2. Every propositional variable pi\Obj p_isource is an atomic formula.

  3. If A!Asource and B!Bsource are formulas, then (A∧B)(!A \land !B)source is a formula.

  4. If A!Asource and B!Bsource are formulas, then (A∨B)(!A \lor !B)source is a formula.

  5. If A!Asource and B!Bsource are formulas, then (A→B)(!A \lif !B)source is a formula.

  6. Nothing else is a formula.

In addition to the primitive connectives introduced above, we also use the following defined symbols: ¬\lnotsource (negation) and ↔\liffsource (biconditional). Formulas constructed using the defined operators are to be understood as follows:

  1. ¬A\lnot !Asource abbreviates A→⊥!A \lif \lfalsesource.

  2. A↔B!A \liff !Bsource abbreviates (A→B)∧(B→A)(!A \lif !B) \land (!B \lif !A)source.

Although ¬\lnotsource is officially treated as an abbreviation, we will sometimes give explicit rules and clauses in definitions for ¬\lnotsource as if it were primitive. This is mostly so we can state practice problems.

The Brouwer--Heyting--Kolmogorov Interpretation

Editorial

Proofs of validity of intuitionistic propositions using the BHK interpretation are confusing; they have to be explained better.

There is an informal constructive interpretation of the intuitionist connectives, usually known as the Brouwer--Heyting--Kolmogorov interpretation. It uses the notion of a “construction,” which you may think of as a constructive proof. (We don't use “proof” in the BHK interpretation so as not to get confused with the notion of a derivation in a formal derivation system.) Based on this intuitive notion, the BHK interpretation explains the meanings of the intuitionistic connectives.

  1. We assume that we know what constitutes a construction of an atomic statement.

  2. A construction of A1∧A2!A_1 \land !A_2source is a pair ⟨M1,M2⟩\tuple{M_1, M_2}source where M1M_1source is a construction of A1!A_1source and M2M_2source is a construction of A2!A_2source.

  3. A construction of A1∨A2!A_1 \lor !A_2source is a pair ⟨s,M⟩\tuple{s, M}source where sssource is 11source and MMsource is a construction of A1!A_1source, or sssource is 22source and MMsource is a construction of A2!A_2source.

  4. A construction of A→B!A \lif !Bsource is a function that converts a construction of A!Asource into a construction of B!Bsource.

  5. There is no construction for ⊥\lfalsesource (absurdity).

  6. ¬A\lnot !Asource is defined as synonym for A→⊥!A \lif \lfalsesource. That is, a construction of ¬A\lnot !Asource is a function converting a construction of A!Asource into a construction of ⊥\lfalsesource.

BHK construction of not falsity

Take ¬⊥\lnot \lfalsesource for example. A construction of it is a function which, given any construction of ⊥\lfalsesource as input, provides a construction of ⊥\lfalsesource as output. Obviously, the identity function Id\Id{}source is such a construction: given a construction MMsource of ⊥\lfalsesource, Id(M)=M\Id{}(M) = Msource yields a construction of ⊥\lfalsesource.

Generally speaking, ¬A\lnot !Asource means “A construction of A!Asource is impossible”.

BHK construction of double-negation introduction

Let us prove A→¬¬A!A \lif \lnot \lnot !Asource for any proposition A!Asource, which is A→((A→⊥)→⊥)!A \lif ((!A \lif \lfalse) \lif \lfalse)source. We can define ggsource as follows: apply the input hhsource to the construction MMsource of A!Asource (that we received earlier). The statement A∨¬A!A \lor \lnot !Asource is called the Law of Excluded Middle. We can prove it for some specific A!Asource (e.g., ⊥∨¬⊥\lfalse \lor \lnot \lfalsesource), but not in general. As you can see, using the BHK interpretation to show the intuitionistic validity of formulas quickly becomes cumbersome and confusing. Luckily, there are better derivation systems for intuitionistic logic, and more precise semantic interpretations.

Natural Deduction

Natural deduction without the ⊥C\FalseClsource rules is a standard derivation system for intuitionistic logic. We repeat the rules here and indicate the motivation using the BHK interpretation. In each case, we can think of a rule which allows us to conclude that if the premises have constructions, so does the conclusion.

Since natural deduction derivations have undischarged assumptions, we should consider such a derivation, say, of A!Asource from undischarged assumptions Γ\Gammasource, as a function that turns constructions of all B∈Γ!B \in \Gammasource into a construction of A!Asource. If there is a derivation of A!Asource from no undischarged assumptions, then there is a construction of A!Asource in the sense of the BHK interpretation. For the purpose of the discussion, however, we'll suppress the Γ\Gammasource when not needed.

An assumption A!Asource by itself is a derivation of A!Asource from the undischarged assumption A!Asource. This agrees with the BHK-interpretation: the identity function on constructions turns any construction of A!Asource into a construction of A!Asource.

Proposition embedding intuitionistic natural deduction in classical logic

If Γ⊢A\Gamma \Proves !Asource in natural deduction for intuitionistic logic, Γ⊢A\Gamma \Proves !Asource in classical logic. In particular, if A!Asource is an intuitionistic theorem, it is also a classical theorem.

Proof

Every natural deduction rule is also a rule in classical natural deduction, so every derivation in intuitionistic logic is also a derivation in classical logic.

Axiomatic derivation

Axiomatic derivations for intuitionistic propositional logic are the conceptually simplest, and historically first, derivation systems. They work just as in classical propositional logic.
