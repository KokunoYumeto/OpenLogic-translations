Chapter. Semantics. Editorial note. This chapter collects definitions for semantics for intuitionistic logic. So far only Kripke and topological semantics are covered. There are no examples yet, either of how models make formulas true or of proofs that formulas are valid. End editorial note.

Section. Introduction. No logic is satisfactorily described without a semantics, and intuitionistic logic is no exception. Whereas for classical logic, the semantics based on valuations is canonical, there are several competing semantics for intuitionistic logic. None of them are completely satisfactory in the sense that they give an intuitionistically acceptable account of the meanings of the connectives. The semantics based on relational models, similar to the semantics for modal logics, is perhaps the most popular one. In this semantics, propositional variables are assigned to worlds, and these worlds are related by an accessibility relation. That relation is always a partial order, that is, it is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. Intuitively, you might think of these worlds as states of knowledge or “evidentiary situations.” A state w prime is accessible from w if and only if, for all we know, w prime is a possible (future) state of knowledge, that is, one that is compatible with what's known at w . Once a proposition is known, it can't become un-known, that is, whenever A is known at w and world w prime is accessible from world w , A is known at w prime as well. So “knowledge” is monotonic with respect to the accessibility relation. If we define “ A is known” as in epistemic logic as “true in all epistemic alternatives,” then A and B is known at w if in all epistemic alternatives, both A and B are known. But since knowledge is monotonic and R is reflexive, that means that A and B is known at w if and only if A and B are known at w . For the same reason, A or B is known at w if and only if at least one of them is known. So for conjunction and disjunction , the truth conditions of the connectives coincide with those in classical logic. The truth conditions for the conditional, however, differ from classical logic. if A, then B is known at w if and only if at no w prime with world w prime is accessible from world w , A is known without B also being known. This is not the same as the condition that A is unknown or B is known at w . For if we know neither A nor B at w , there might be a future epistemic state w prime with world w prime is accessible from world w such that at w prime , A is known without also coming to know B . We know not A only if there is no possible future epistemic state in which we know A . Here the idea is that if A were knowable, then in some possible future epistemic state A becomes known. Since we can't know falsity , in that future epistemic state, we would know A but not know falsity . On this interpretation the principle of excluded middle fails. For there are some A which we don't yet know, but which we might come to know. For such a formula A , both A and not A are unknown, so A or not A is not known. But we do know, for example, that not both A and not A . For no future state in which we know both A and not A is possible, and we know this independently of whether or not we know A or not A . Relational models are not the only available semantics for intuitionistic logic. The topological semantics is another: here propositions are interpreted as open sets in a topological space, and the connectives are interpreted as operations on these sets (for example, conjunction corresponds to intersection). End section.
