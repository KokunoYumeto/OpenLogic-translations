Section. Overview. The lambda calculus was originally designed by Alonzo Church in the early nineteen thirties as a basis for constructive logic, and not as a model of the computable functions. But it was soon shown to be equivalent to other definitions of computability, such as the Turing computable functions and the partial recursive functions. The fact that this initially came as a small surprise makes the characterization all the more interesting. Lambda notation is a convenient way of referring to a function directly by a symbolic expression which defines it, instead of defining a name for it. Instead of saying “let f be the function defined by f of x equals x plus three ,” one can say, “let f be the function lambda x, with body x plus three .” In other words, lambda x, with body x plus three is just a name for the function that adds three to its argument. In this expression, x is a dummy variable, or a placeholder: the same function can just as well be denoted by lambda y, with body y plus three . The notation works even with other parameters around. For example, suppose g of x and y is a function of two variables, and k is a natural number. Then lambda x, with body g of x and k is the function which maps any x to g of x and k . This way of defining a function from a symbolic expression is known as lambda abstraction. The flip side of lambda abstraction is application: assuming one has a function f (say, defined on the natural numbers), one can apply it to any value, like two. In conventional notation, of course, we write f of two for the result. What happens when you combine lambda abstraction with application? Then the resulting expression can be simplified, by “plugging” the applicand in for the abstracted variable. For example, Displayed expression. the abstraction lambda x, with body x plus three, applied to two. End displayed expression. can be simplified to two plus three . Up to this point, we have done nothing but introduce new notations for conventional notions. The lambda calculus, however, represents a more radical departure from the set-theoretic viewpoint. In this framework: List. Next item. Everything denotes a function. Next item. Functions can be defined using lambda abstraction. Next item. Anything can be applied to anything else. End list. For example, if capital F is a term in the lambda calculus, capital F applied to itself is always assumed to be meaningful. This liberal framework is known as the untyped lambda calculus, where “untyped” means “no restriction on what can be applied to what.” Digression. There is also a typed lambda calculus, which is an important variation on the untyped version. Although in many ways the typed lambda calculus is similar to the untyped one, it is much easier to reconcile with a classical set-theoretic framework, and has some very different properties. Research on the lambda calculus has proved to be central in theoretical computer science, and in the design of programming languages. LISP, designed by John McCarthy in the nineteen fifties, is an early example of a language that was influenced by these ideas. End digression. End section.

Section. The Church—Rosser Property. Theorem. Let capital M , capital N subscript one , and capital N subscript two be terms, such that capital M beta reduces to capital N subscript one and capital M beta reduces to capital N subscript two . Then there is a term capital P such that capital N subscript one beta reduces to capital P and capital N subscript two beta reduces to capital P . End theorem. Corollary. Suppose capital M can be reduced to normal form. Then this normal form is unique. End corollary. Proof. If capital M beta reduces to capital N subscript one and capital M beta reduces to capital N subscript two , by the previous theorem there is a term capital P such that capital N subscript one and capital N subscript two both reduce to capital P . If capital N subscript one and capital N subscript two are both in normal form, this can only happen if capital N subscript one, capital P, and capital N subscript two are the same term, under the chapter's bound variable renaming convention . End proof. Finally, we will say that two terms capital M and capital N are beta -equivalent, or just equivalent, if they reduce to a common term; in other words, if there is some capital P such that capital M beta reduces to capital P and capital N beta reduces to capital P . This is written capital M is beta equivalent to capital N . Using the Church Rosser theorem , you can check that beta equivalence is an equivalence relation, with the additional property that for every capital M and capital N , if capital M beta reduces to capital N or capital N beta reduces to capital M , then capital M is beta equivalent to capital N . (In fact, one can show that beta equivalence is the smallest equivalence relation having this property.) End section.

Section. Currying. A lambda -abstract lambda x, with body capital M represents a function of one argument, which is quite a limitation when we want to define function accepting multiple arguments. One way to do this would be by extending the lambda -calculus to allow the formation of pairs, triples, etc., in which case, say, a three-place function lambda x, with body capital M would expect its argument to be a triple. However, it is more convenient to do this by Currying. Let's consider an example. We'll pretend for a moment that we have a plus operation in the lambda -calculus. The addition function is two place , that is, it takes two arguments. But a lambda -abstract only gives us functions of one argument: the syntax does not allow expressions like the proposed abstraction lambda, binding the pair x comma y, with body x plus y . However, we can consider the one-place function f subscript x applied to y given by lambda y, with body x plus y , which adds x to its single argument y . Actually, this is not a single function, but a family of different functions “add x ,” one for each number x . Now we can define another one-place function g as lambda x, with body f subscript x . Applied to argument x , g of x returns the function f subscript x —so its values are other functions. Now if we apply g to x , and then the result to y we get: g of x applied to y equals f subscript x of y, which equals x plus y . In this way, the one-place function g can do the same job as the two-place addition function. “Currying” simply refers to this trick for turning two-place functions into one place functions (whose values are one-place functions). Section. Lambda-definable Functions are Computable. Theorem. If a partial function f is lambda-defined by a lambda term, it is computable. End theorem. Proof. Suppose a function f is lambda-defined by a lambda term capital X . Let us describe an informal procedure to compute f . On input m subscript zero , and so on, m subscript n minus one , write down the term capital X applied successively to the Church numerals for m subscript zero through m subscript n minus one . Build a tree, first writing down all the one-step reductions of the original term; below that, write all the one-step reductions of those (that is, the two-step reductions of the original term); and keep going. If you ever reach a numeral, return that as the answer; otherwise, the function is undefined. An appeal to Church's thesis tells us that this function is computable. A better way to prove the theorem would be to give a recursive description of this search procedure. For example, one could define a sequence primitive recursive functions and relations, “ Is A Subterm ,” “ Substitute ,” “ Reduces To In One Step ,” “ Reduction Sequence ,” “ Numeral ,” etc. The partial recursive procedure for computing f of m subscript zero through m subscript n minus one is then to search for a sequence of one-step reductions starting with capital X applied successively to the Church numerals for m subscript zero through m subscript n minus one and ending with a numeral, and return the number corresponding to that numeral. The details are long and tedious but otherwise routine. End proof. End section.
