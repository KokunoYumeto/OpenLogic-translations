Section. Introduction. At first glance, the lambda calculus is just a very abstract calculus of expressions that represent functions and applications of them to others. Nothing in the syntax of the lambda calculus suggests that these are functions of particular kinds of objects, in particular, the syntax includes no mention of natural numbers. Its basic operations—application and lambda abstractions—are operations that apply to any function, not just functions on natural numbers. Nevertheless, with some ingenuity, it is possible to define arithmetical functions, that is, functions on the natural numbers, in the lambda calculus. To do this, we define, for each natural number n is a natural number , a special lambda -term the Church numeral for n , the Church numeral for n . (Church numerals are named for Alonzo Church.) End displayed expression. Here, the result of iterating f n times on x, end iteration stands for the result of applying f to x n times. For example, the Church numeral for zero is lambda f then x, with body x, end abstraction , and the Church numeral for three is lambda f then x, with body the application of f to the application of f to the application of f to x, end application, end application, end application, end abstraction . End definition. The Church numeral the Church numeral for n is encoded as a lambda term which represents a function accepting two arguments f and x , and returns the result of iterating f n times on x, end iteration . Church numerals are evidently in normal form. A representation of natural numbers in the lambda calculus is only useful, of course, if we can compute with them. Computing with Church numerals in the lambda calculus means applying a lambda -term capital F to such a Church numeral, and reducing the combined term the application of capital F to the Church numeral for n, end application to a normal form. If it always reduces to a normal form, and the normal form is always a Church numeral the Church numeral for m , we can think of the output of the computation as being the number m . We can then think of capital F as defining a function f from the natural numbers to the natural numbers , namely the function such that f of n equals m if and only if the application of capital F to the Church numeral for n, end application reduces to the Church numeral for m . Because of the Church—Rosser property, normal forms are unique if they exist. So if the application of capital F to the Church numeral for n, end application reduces to the Church numeral for m , there can be no other term in normal form, in particular no other Church numeral, that the application of capital F to the Church numeral for n, end application reduces to. Conversely, given a function f from the natural numbers to the natural numbers , we can ask if there is a term capital F that defines f in this way.
