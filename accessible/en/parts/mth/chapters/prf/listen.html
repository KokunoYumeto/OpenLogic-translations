Chapter. Proofs.

Section. Introduction. Based on your experiences in introductory logic, you might be comfortable with a derivation system—probably a natural deduction or Fitch style derivation system, or perhaps a proof-tree system. You probably remember doing proofs in these systems, either proving a formula or show that a given argument is valid. In order to do this, you applied the rules of the system until you got the desired end result. In reasoning about logic, we also prove things, but in most cases we are not using a derivation system. In fact, most of the proofs we consider are done in English (perhaps, with some symbolic language thrown in) rather than entirely in the language of first-order logic. When constructing such proofs, you might at first be at a loss—how do I prove something without a derivation system? How do I start? How do I know if my proof is correct? Before attempting a proof, it's important to know what a proof is and how to construct one. As implied by the name, a proof is meant to show that something is true. You might think of this in terms of a dialogue—someone asks you if something is true, say, if every prime other than two is an odd number. To answer “yes” is not enough; they might want to know why. In this case, you'd give them a proof. In everyday discourse, it might be enough to gesture at an answer, or give an incomplete answer. In logic and mathematics, however, we want rigorous proof—we want to show that something is true beyond any doubt. This means that every step in our proof must be justified, and the justification must be cogent (that is, the assumption you're using is actually assumed in the statement of the theorem you're proving, the definitions you apply must be correctly applied, the justifications appealed to must be correct inferences, etc.). Usually, we're proving some statement. We call the statements we're proving by various names: propositions, theorems, lemmas, or corollaries. A proposition is a basic proof-worthy statement: important enough to record, but perhaps not particularly deep nor applied often. A theorem is a significant, important proposition. Its proof often is broken into several steps, and sometimes it is named after the person who first proved it (for example, Cantor's Theorem, the Loewenheim—Skolem theorem) or after the fact it concerns (for example, the completeness theorem). A lemma is a proposition or theorem that is used in the proof of a more important result. Confusingly, sometimes lemmas are important results in themselves, and also named after the person who introduced them (for example, Zorn's Lemma). A corollary is a result that easily follows from another one. A statement to be proved often contains assumptions that clarify which kinds of things we're proving something about. It might begin with “Let formula A be a formula of the form formula B implies formula C ” or “Suppose Gamma proves formula A ” or something of the sort. These are hypotheses of the proposition, theorem, or lemma, and you may assume these to be true in your proof. They restrict what we're proving, and also introduce some names for the objects we're talking about. For instance, if your proposition begins with “Let formula A be a formula of the form formula B implies formula C ,” you're proving something about all formulas of a certain sort only (namely, conditionals), and it's understood that formula B implies formula C is an arbitrary conditional that your proof will talk about. End section.

Section. Starting a Proof. But where do you even start? You've been given something to prove, so this should be the last thing that is mentioned in the proof (you can, obviously, announce that you're going to prove it at the beginning, but you don't want to use it as an assumption). Write what you are trying to prove at the bottom of a fresh sheet of paper—this way you don't lose sight of your goal. Next, you may have some assumptions that you are able to use (this will be made clearer when we talk about the type of proof you are doing in the next section). Write these at the top of the page and make sure to flag that they are assumptions (that is, if you are assuming claim p , write “assume that claim p ,” or “suppose that claim p ”). Finally, there might be some definitions in the question that you need to know. You might be told to use a specific definition, or there might be various definitions in the assumptions or conclusion that you are working towards. Write these down and ensure that you understand what they mean. How you set up your proof will also be dependent upon the form of the question. The next section provides details on how to set up your proof based on the type of sentence. End section.

Section. Using Definitions. We mentioned that you must be familiar with all definitions that may be used in the proof, and that you can properly apply them. This is a really important point, and it is worth looking at in a bit more detail. Definitions are used to abbreviate properties and relations so we can talk about them more succinctly. The introduced abbreviation is called the definiendum, and what it abbreviates is the definiens. In proofs, we often have to go back to how the definiendum was introduced, because we have to exploit the logical structure of the definiens (the long version of which the defined term is the abbreviation) to get through our proof. By unpacking definitions, you're ensuring that you're getting to the heart of where the logical action is. We'll start with an example. Suppose you want to prove the following: Proposition. For any sets A and set B , the union of set A and set B equals the union of set B and set A . End proposition. In order to even start the proof, we need to know what it means for two sets to be identical; that is, we need to know what the “ set equality ” in that equation means for sets. Sets are defined to be identical whenever they have the same elements. So the definition we have to unpack is: Definition. Sets A and set B are identical, set A equals set B , if and only if every element of set A is an element of set B , and vice versa. End definition. This definition uses set A and set B as placeholders for arbitrary sets. What it defines—the definiendum —is the expression “ set A equals set B ” by giving the condition under which set A equals set B is true. This condition—“every element of set A is an element of set B , and vice versa”—is the definiens. Footnote. In this particular case—and very confusingly!—when set A equals set B , the sets A and set B are just one and the same set, even though we use different letters for it on the left and the right side. But the ways in which that set is picked out may be different, and that makes the definition non-trivial. End footnote. The definition specifies that set A equals set B is true if, and only if (we abbreviate this to “if and only if”) the condition holds. When you apply the definition, you have to match the set A and set B in the definition to the case you're dealing with. In our case, it means that in order for the union of set A and set B equals the union of set B and set A to be true, each z is an element of the union of set A and set B must also be in the union of set B and set A , and vice versa. The expression the union of set A and set B in the proposition plays the role of set A in the definition, and the union of set B and set A that of set B . Since set A and set B are used both in the definition and in the statement of the proposition we're proving, but in different uses, you have to be careful to make sure you don't mix up the two. For instance, it would be a mistake to think that you could prove the proposition by showing that every element of set A is an element of set B , and vice versa—that would show that set A equals set B , not that the union of set A and set B equals the union of set B and set A . (Also, since set A and set B may be any two sets, you won't get very far, because if nothing is assumed about set A and set B they may well be different sets.) Within the proof we are dealing with set-theoretic notions such as union, and so we must also know the meanings of the symbol union in order to understand how the proof should proceed. And sometimes, unpacking the definition gives rise to further definitions to unpack. For instance, the union of set A and set B is defined as the set of all z such that z is an element of set A or z is an element of set B . So if you want to prove that x is an element of the union of set A and set B , unpacking the definition of union tells you that you have to prove x is an element of the set of all z such that z is an element of set A or z is an element of set B . Now you also have to remember that x is an element of the set of all z satisfying the indicated condition involving z if and only if the indicated condition involving x . So, further unpacking the definition of the the set of all z satisfying the indicated condition involving z notation, what you have to show is: x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B . So, “every element of the union of set A and set B is also an element of the union of set B and set A ” really means: “for every x , if x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B , then x is an element of set B or x is an element of set A .” If we fully unpack the definitions in the proposition, we see that what we have to show is this: Proposition. For any sets A and set B : (a) for every x , if x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B , then x is an element of set B or x is an element of set A , and (b) for every x , if x is an element of set B or x is an element of set A , then x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B . End proposition. What's important is that unpacking definitions is a necessary part of constructing a proof. Properly doing it is sometimes difficult: you must be careful to distinguish and match the variables in the definition and the terms in the claim you're proving. In order to be successful, you must know what the question is asking and what all the terms used in the question mean—you will often need to unpack more than one definition. In simple proofs such as the ones below, the solution follows almost immediately from the definitions themselves. Of course, it won't always be this simple. Exercise. Suppose you are asked to prove that the intersection of set A and set B is not equal to the empty set . Unpack all the definitions occurring here, that is, restate this in a way that does not mention “ intersection ”, “ the equality sign ”, or “ the empty set ”. End exercise. This exercise remains unsolved. End section.

Section. Inference Patterns. Proofs are composed of individual inferences. When we make an inference, we typically indicate that by using a word like “so,” “thus,” or “therefore.” The inference often relies on one or two facts we already have available in our proof—it may be something we have assumed, or something that we've concluded by an inference already. To be clear, we may label these things, and in the inference we indicate what other statements we're using in the inference. An inference will often also contain an explanation of why our new conclusion follows from the things that come before it. There are some common patterns of inference that are used very often in proofs; we'll go through some below. Some patterns of inference, like proofs by induction, are more involved (and will be discussed later). We've already discussed one pattern of inference: unpacking, or applying, a definition. When we unpack a definition, we just restate something that involves the definiendum by using the definiens. For instance, suppose that we have already established in the course of a proof that set D equals set E (a). Then we may apply the definition of set equality for sets and infer: “Thus, by definition from (a), every element of set D is an element of set E and vice versa.” Somewhat confusingly, we often do not write the justification of an inference when we actually make it, but before. Suppose we haven't already proved that set D equals set E , but we want to. If set D equals set E is the conclusion we aim for, then we can restate this aim also by applying the definition: to prove set D equals set E we have to prove that every element of set D is an element of set E and vice versa. So our proof will have the form: (a) prove that every element of set D is an element of set E ; (b) every element of set E is an element of set D ; (c) therefore, from (a) and (b) by definition of set equality , set D equals set E . But we would usually not write it this way. Instead we might write something like, Quotation. We want to show set D equals set E . By definition of set equality , this amounts to showing that every element of set D is an element of set E and vice versa. (a) and so on (a proof that every element of set D is an element of set E ) and so on (b) and so on (a proof that every element of set E is an element of set D ) and so on End quotation. Section. Using a Conjunction. Perhaps the simplest inference pattern is that of drawing as conclusion one of the conjuncts of a conjunction. In other words: if we have assumed or already proved that claim p and claim q , then we're entitled to infer that claim p (and also that claim q ). This is such a basic inference that it is often not mentioned. For instance, once we've unpacked the definition of set D equals set E we've established that every element of set D is an element of set E and vice versa. From this we can conclude that every element of set E is an element of set D (that's the “vice versa” part). Section. Proving a Conjunction. Sometimes what you'll be asked to prove will have the form of a conjunction; you will be asked to “prove claim p and claim q .” In this case, you simply have to do two things: prove claim p , and then prove claim q . You could divide your proof into two sections, and for clarity, label them. When you're making your first notes, you might write “(one) Prove claim p ” at the top of the page, and “(two) Prove claim q ” in the middle of the page. (Of course, you might not be explicitly asked to prove a conjunction but find that your proof requires that you prove a conjunction. For instance, if you're asked to prove that set D equals set E you will find that, after unpacking the definition of set equality , you have to prove: every element of set D is an element of set E and every element of set E is an element of set D ). Section. Proving a Disjunction. When what you are proving takes the form of a disjunction (that is, it is an statement of the form “ claim p or claim q ”), it is enough to show that one of the disjuncts is true. However, it basically never happens that either disjunct just follows from the assumptions of your theorem. More often, the assumptions of your theorem are themselves disjunctive, or you're showing that all things of a certain kind have one of two properties, but some of the things have the one and others have the other property. This is where proof by cases is useful (see below). Section. Conditional Proof. Many theorems you will encounter are in conditional form (that is, show that if claim p holds, then claim q is also true). These cases are nice and easy to set up—simply assume the antecedent of the conditional (in this case, claim p ) and prove the conclusion claim q from it. So if your theorem reads, “If claim p then claim q ,” you start your proof with “assume claim p ” and at the end you should have proved claim q . Conditionals may be stated in different ways. So instead of “If claim p then claim q ,” a theorem may state that “ claim p only if claim q ,” “ claim q if claim p ,” or “ claim q , provided claim p .” These all mean the same and require assuming claim p and proving claim q from that assumption. Recall that a biconditional (“ claim p if and only if (if and only if) claim q ”) is really two conditionals put together: if claim p then claim q , and if claim q then claim p . All you have to do, then, is two instances of conditional proof: one for the first conditional and another one for the second. Sometimes, however, it is possible to prove an “if and only if” statement by chaining together a bunch of other “if and only if” statements so that you start with “ claim p ” an end with “ claim q ”—but in that case you have to make sure that each step really is an “if and only if.” Section. Universal Claims. Using a universal claim is simple: if something is true for anything, it's true for each particular thing. So if, say, the hypothesis of your proof is set A is a subset of set B , that means (unpacking the definition of the subset relation ), that, for every x is an element of set A , x is an element of set B . Thus, if you already know that z is an element of set A , you can conclude z is an element of set B . Proving a universal claim may seem a little bit tricky. Usually these statements take the following form: “If x has property P , then it has property Q ” or “All property P s are property Q s.” Of course, it might not fit this form perfectly, and it takes a bit of practice to figure out what you're asked to prove exactly. But: we often have to prove that all objects with some property have a certain other property. The way to prove a universal claim is to introduce names or variables, for the things that have the one property and then show that they also have the other property. We might put this by saying that to prove something for all property P s you have to prove it for an arbitrary property P . And the name introduced is a name for an arbitrary property P . We typically use single letters as these names for arbitrary things, and the letters usually follow conventions: for example, we use n for natural numbers, formula A for formulas, set A for sets, function f for functions, etc. The trick is to maintain generality throughout the proof. You start by assuming that an arbitrary object (“ x ”) has the property property P , and show (based only on definitions or what you are allowed to assume) that x has the property property Q . Because you have not stipulated what x is specifically, other that it has the property property P , then you can assert that everything with property P has the property property Q . In short, x is a stand-in for all things with property property P . Proposition. For all sets A and set B , set A is a subset of the union of set A and set B . End proposition. Proof. Let set A and set B be arbitrary sets. We want to show that set A is a subset of the union of set A and set B . By definition of the subset relation , this amounts to: for every x , if x is an element of set A then x is an element of the union of set A and set B . So let x is an element of set A be an arbitrary element of set A . We have to show that x is an element of the union of set A and set B . Since x is an element of set A , x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B . Thus, x is an element of the set of all x such that x is an element of set A or x is an element of set B . But that, by definition of union , means x is an element of the union of set A and set B . End proof. Section. Proof by Cases. Suppose you have a disjunction as an assumption or as an already established conclusion—you have assumed or proved that claim p or claim q is true. You want to prove claim r . You do this in two steps: first you assume that claim p is true, and prove claim r , then you assume that claim q is true and prove claim r again. This works because we assume or know that one of the two alternatives holds. The two steps establish that either one is sufficient for the truth of claim r . (If both are true, we have not one but two reasons for why claim r is true. It is not necessary to separately prove that claim r is true assuming both claim p and claim q .) To indicate what we're doing, we announce that we “distinguish cases.” For instance, suppose we know that x is an element of the union of set B and set C . the union of set B and set C is defined as the set of all x such that x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C . In other words, by definition, x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C . We would prove that x is an element of set A from this by first assuming that x is an element of set B , and proving x is an element of set A from this assumption, and then assume x is an element of set C , and again prove x is an element of set A from this. You would write “We distinguish cases” under the assumption, then “Case (one): x is an element of set B ” underneath, and “Case (two): x is an element of set C halfway down the page. Then you'd proceed to fill in the top half and the bottom half of the page. Proof by cases is especially useful if what you're proving is itself disjunctive. Here's a simple example: Proposition. Suppose set B is a subset of set D and set C is a subset of set E . Then the union of set B and set C is a subset of the union of set D and set E . End proposition. Proof. Assume (a) that set B is a subset of set D and (b) set C is a subset of set E . By definition, any x is an element of set B is also is an element of set D (c) and any x is an element of set C is also is an element of set E (d). To show that the union of set B and set C is a subset of the union of set D and set E , we have to show that if x is an element of the union of set B and set C then x is an element of the union of set D and set E (by definition of the subset relation ). x is an element of the union of set B and set C if and only if x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C (by definition of union ). Similarly, x is an element of the union of set D and set E if and only if x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . So, we have to show: for any x , if x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C , then x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . Quotation. So far we've only unpacked definitions! We've reformulated our proposition without the subset relation and union and are left with trying to prove a universal conditional claim. By what we've discussed above, this is done by assuming that x is something about which we assume the “if” part is true, and we'll go on to show that the “then” part is true as well. In other words, we'll assume that x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C and show that x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . Footnote. This paragraph just explains what we're doing—it's not part of the proof, and you don't have to go into all this detail when you write down your own proofs. End footnote. End quotation. Suppose that x is an element of set B or x is an element of set C . We have to show that x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . We distinguish cases. Case one: x is an element of set B . By (c), x is an element of set D . Thus, x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . (Here we've made the inference discussed in the preceding subsection!) Case two: x is an element of set C . By (d), x is an element of set E . Thus, x is an element of set D or x is an element of set E . End proof. Section. Proving an Existence Claim. When asked to prove an existence claim, the question will usually be of the form “prove that there is an x such that the indicated condition involving x ”, that is, that some object that has the property described by “ the indicated condition involving x ”. In this case you'll have to identify a suitable object and show that it has the required property. This sounds straightforward, but a proof of this kind can be tricky. Typically it involves constructing or defining an object and proving that the object so defined has the required property. Finding the right object may be hard, proving that it has the required property may be hard, and sometimes it's even tricky to show that you've succeeded in defining an object at all! Generally, you'd write this out by specifying the object, for example, “let x be and so on ” (where and so on specifies which object you have in mind), possibly proving that the indicated omitted material in fact describes an object that exists, and then go on to show that x has the property property Q . Here's a simple example. Proposition. Suppose that x is an element of set B . Then there is an set A such that set A is a subset of set B and set A is not equal to the empty set . End proposition. Proof. Assume x is an element of set B . Let set A equals the singleton set containing x . Quotation. Here we've defined the set A by enumerating its elements. Since we assume that x is an object, and we can always form a set by enumerating its elements, we don't have to show that we've succeeded in defining a set A here. However, we still have to show that set A has the properties required by the proposition. The proof isn't complete without that! End quotation. Since x is an element of set A , set A is not equal to the empty set . Quotation. This relies on the definition of set A as the singleton set containing x and the obvious facts that x is an element of the singleton set containing x and x is not an element of the empty set . End quotation. Since x is the only element of the singleton set containing x , and x is an element of set B , every element of set A is also an element of set B . By definition of the subset relation , set A is a subset of set B . End proof. Section. Using Existence Claims. Suppose you know that some existence claim is true (you've proved it, or it's a hypothesis you can use), say, “for some x , x is an element of set A ” or “there is an x is an element of set A .” If you want to use it in your proof, you can just pretend that you have a name for one of the things which your hypothesis says exist. Since set A contains at least one thing, there are things to which that name might refer. You might of course not be able to pick one out or describe it further (other than that it is is an element of set A ). But for the purpose of the proof, you can pretend that you have picked it out and give a name to it. It's important to pick a name that you haven't already used (or that appears in your hypotheses), otherwise things can go wrong. In your proof, you indicate this by going from “for some x , x is an element of set A ” to “Let object a is an element of set A .” Now you can reason about object a , use some other hypotheses, etc., until you come to a conclusion, claim p . If claim p no longer mentions object a , claim p is independent of the asusmption that object a is an element of set A , and you've shown that it follows just from the assumption “for some x , x is an element of set A .” Proposition. If set A is not equal to the empty set , then the union of set A and set B is not equal to the empty set . End proposition. Proof. Suppose set A is not equal to the empty set . So for some x , x is an element of set A . Quotation. Here we first just restated the hypothesis of the proposition. This hypothesis, that is, set A is not equal to the empty set , hides an existential claim, which you get to only by unpacking a few definitions. The definition of set equality tells us that set A equals the empty set if and only if every x is an element of set A is also is an element of the empty set and every x is an element of the empty set is also is an element of set A . Negating both sides, we get: set A is not equal to the empty set if and only if either some x is an element of set A is is not an element of the empty set or some x is an element of the empty set is is not an element of set A . Since nothing is is an element of the empty set , the second disjunct can never be true, and “ x is an element of set A and x is not an element of the empty set ” reduces to just x is an element of set A . So x is not equal to the empty set if and only if for some x , x is an element of set A . That's an existence claim. Now we use that existence claim by introducing a name for one of the elements of set A : End quotation. Let object a is an element of set A . Quotation. Now we've introduced a name for one of the things is an element of set A . We'll continue to argue about object a , but we'll be careful to only assume that object a is an element of set A and nothing else: End quotation. Since object a is an element of set A , object a is an element of the union of set A and set B , by definition of union . So for some x , x is an element of the union of set A and set B , that is, the union of set A and set B is not equal to the empty set . Quotation. In that last step, we went from “ object a is an element of the union of set A and set B ” to “for some x , x is an element of the union of set A and set B .” That doesn't mention object a anymore, so we know that “for some x , x is an element of the union of set A and set B ” follows from “for some x , x is an element of set A alone.” But that means that the union of set A and set B is not equal to the empty set . End quotation. End proof. Source anomaly note. Source variable caveat. Section. An Example. Our first example is the following simple fact about unions and intersections of sets. It will illustrate unpacking definitions, proofs of conjunctions, of universal claims, and proof by cases. Proposition. For any sets A , set B , and set C , the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C equals the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C End proposition. Let's prove it! Proof. We want to show that for any sets A , set B , and set C , the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C equals the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C Quotation. First we unpack the definition of “ set equality ” in the statement of the proposition. Recall that proving sets identical means showing that the sets have the same elements. That is, all elements of the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C are also elements of the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C , and vice versa. The “vice versa” means that also every element of the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C must be an element of the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C . So in unpacking the definition, we see that we have to prove a conjunction. Let's record this: End quotation. By definition, the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C equals the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C if and only if every element of the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C is also an element of the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C , and every element of the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C is an element of the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C . Quotation. Since this is a conjunction, we must prove each conjunct separately. Lets start with the first: let's prove that every element of the union of set A with the intersection of set B and set C is also an element of the intersection of the union of set A and set B with the union of set A and set C . Section. Proof by Contradiction. In the first instance, proof by contradiction is an inference pattern that is used to prove negative claims. Suppose you want to show that some claim claim p is false, that is, you want to show not claim p . The most promising strategy is to (a) suppose that claim p is true, and (b) show that this assumption leads to something you know to be false. “Something known to be false” may be a result that conflicts with—contradicts— claim p itself, or some other hypothesis of the overall claim you are considering. For instance, a proof of “if claim q then not claim p ” involves assuming that claim q is true and proving not claim p from it. If you prove not claim p by contradiction, that means assuming claim p in addition to claim q . If you can prove not claim q from claim p , you have shown that the assumption claim p leads to something that contradicts your other assumption claim q , since claim q and not claim q cannot both be true. Of course, you have to use other inference patterns in your proof of the contradiction, as well as unpacking definitions. Let's consider an example. Proposition. We can make that a bit more explicit: it's not the case that there is an x is an element of set A . End quotation. set A has no elements if and only if it's not the case that there is an x such that x is an element of set A . Quotation. Section. Reading Proofs. Proofs you find in textbooks and articles very seldom give all the details we have so far included in our examples. Authors often do not draw attention to when they distinguish cases, when they give an indirect proof, or don't mention that they use a definition. So when you read a proof in a textbook, you will often have to fill in those details for yourself in order to understand the proof. Doing this is also good practice to get the hang of the various moves you have to make in a proof. Let's look at an example. Proposition. Absorption. The proposition proved is a general claim about any sets A and set B , and when the proof mentions set A or set B , these are variables for arbitrary sets. The general claims the proof establishes is what's required to prove identity of sets, that is, that every element of the left side of the identity is an element of the right and vice versa. Quotation. “If z is an element of the intersection of set A with the union of set A and set B , then z is an element of set A , so the intersection of set A with the union of set A and set B is a subset of set A .” End quotation. Section. I Can't Do It!. We all get to a point where we feel like giving up. But you can do it. Your instructor and teaching assistant, as well as your fellow students, can help. Ask them for help! Here are a few tips to help you avoid a crisis, and what to do if you feel like giving up. To make sure you can solve problems successfully, do the following: List. Next item. Start as far in advance as possible. We get busy throughout the semester and many of us struggle with procrastination, one of the best things you can do is to start your homework assignments early. That way, if you're stuck, you have time to look for a solution (that isn't crying). Next item. Talk to your classmates. You are not alone. Others in the class may also struggle—but they may struggle with different things. Talking it out with your peers can give you a different perspective on the problem that might lead to a breakthrough. Of course, don't just copy their solution: ask them for a hint, or explain where you get stuck and ask them for the next step. And when you do get it, reciprocate. Helping someone else along, and explaining things will help you understand better, too. Next item. Ask for help. You have many resources available to you—your instructor and teaching assistant are there for you and want you to succeed. They should be able to help you work out a problem and identify where in the process you're struggling. Next item. Take a break. If you're stuck, it might be because you've been staring at the problem for too long. Take a short break, have a cup of tea, or work on a different problem for a while, then return to the problem with a fresh mind. Sleep on it. End list. Notice how these strategies require that you've started to work on the proof well in advance? If you've started the proof at twoam the day before it's due, these might not be so helpful. This might sound like doom and gloom, but solving a proof is a challenge that pays off in the end. Some people do this as a career—so there must be something to enjoy about it. Like basically everything, solving problems and doing proofs is something that requires practice. You might see classmates who find this easy: they've probably just had lots of practice already. Try not to give in too easily. If you do run out of time (or patience) on a particular problem: that's ok. It doesn't mean you're stupid or that you will never get it. Find out (from your instructor or another student) how it is done, and identify where you went wrong or got stuck, so you can avoid doing that the next time you encounter a similar issue. Then try to do it without looking at the solution. And next time, start (and ask for help) earlier. End section.
