Chapter. Syntax and Semantics.

Section. Introduction. Modal logic deals with modal propositions and the entailment relations among them. Examples of modal propositions are the following: List. Next item. It is necessary that two plus two equals four . Next item. It is necessarily possible that it will rain tomorrow. Next item. If it is necessarily possible that formula A then it is possible that formula A . End list. Possibility and necessity are not the only modalities: other unary connectives are also classified as modalities, for instance, “it ought to be the case that formula A ,” “It will be the case that formula A ,” “Dana knows that formula A ,” or “Dana believes that formula A .” Modal logic makes its first appearance in Aristotle's De Interpretatione: he was the first to notice that necessity implies possibility, but not vice versa; that possibility and necessity are inter-definable; that If A and B is possibly true then formula A is possibly true and formula B is possibly true, but not conversely; and that if A implies B is necessary, then if formula A is necessary, so is formula B . The first modern approach to modal logic was the work of C. I. Lewis, culminating with Lewis and Langford, Symbolic Logic (one thousand nine hundred thirty two). Lewis and Langford were unhappy with the representation of implication by means of the material conditional: if A, then B is a poor substitute for “ formula A implies formula B .” Instead, they proposed to characterize implication as “Necessarily, if formula A then formula B ,” symbolized as necessarily, if A then B . In trying to sort out the different properties, Lewis identified five different modal systems, Sone, and so on, Sfour, Sfive, the last two of which are still in use. The approach of Lewis and Langford was purely syntactical: they identified reasonable axioms and rules and investigated what was provable with those means. A semantic approach remained elusive for a long time, until a first attempt was made by Rudolf Carnap in Meaning and Necessity (one thousand nine hundred forty seven) using the notion of a state description, that is, a collection of atomic sentences (those that are “true” in that state description). After lifting the truth definition to arbitrary sentences formula A , Carnap defines formula A to be necessarily true if it is true in all state descriptions. Carnap's approach could not handle iterated modalities, in that sentences of the form “Possibly necessarily and so on possibly formula A ” always reduce to the innermost modality. The major breakthrough in modal semantics came with Saul Kripke's article “A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic” (JSL one thousand nine hundred fifty nine). Kripke based his work on Leibniz's idea that a statement is necessarily true if it is true “at all possible worlds.” This idea, though, suffers from the same drawbacks as Carnap's, in that the truth of statement at a world w (or a state description s ) does not depend on w at all. So Kripke assumed that worlds are related by an accessibility relation R , and that a statement of the form “Necessarily formula A ” is true at a world w if and only if formula A is true at all worlds w prime accessible from w . Semantics that provide some version of this approach are called Kripke semantics and made possible the tumultuous development of modal logics (in the plural). When interpreted by the Kripke semantics, modal logic shows us what relational structures look like “from the inside.” A relational structure is just a set equipped with a binary relation (for instance, the set of students in the class ordered by their social security number is a relational structure). But in fact relational structures come in all sorts of domains: besides relative possibility of states of the world, we can have epistemic states of some agent related by epistemic possibility, or states of a dynamical system with their state transitions, etc. Modal logic can be used to model all of these: the first gives us ordinary, alethic, modal logic; the others give us epistemic logic, dynamic logic, etc. We focus on one particular angle, known to modal logicians as “correspondence theory.” One of the most significant early discoveries of Kripke's is that many properties of the accessibility relation R (whether it is transitive, symmetric, etc.) can be characterized in the modal language itself by means of appropriate “modal schemas.” Modal logicians say, for instance, that the reflexivity of R “corresponds” to the schema “If necessarily formula A , then formula A ”. We explore mainly the correspondence theory of a number of classical systems of modal logic (for example, Sfour and Sfive) obtained by a combination of the schemas axiom D, axiom T, axiom B, axiom four, and axiom five. End section.

Section. Validity. Formulas that are true in all models, that is, true at every world in every model, are particularly interesting. They represent those modal propositions which are true regardless of how necessity and possibility are interpreted, as long as the interpretation is “normal” in the sense that it is generated by some accessibility relation on possible worlds. We call such formulas valid. For instance, if the conjunction of p and q is necessary, then p is necessary is valid. Some formulas one might expect to be valid on the basis of the alethic interpretation of necessity , such as if necessarily p, then p , are not valid, however. Part of the interest of relational models is that different interpretations of necessity and possibility can be captured by different kinds of accessibility relations. This suggests that we should define validity not just relative to all models, but relative to all models of a certain kind. It will turn out, for example, that if necessarily p, then p is true in all models where every world is accessible from itself, that is, R is reflexive. Section. Entailment. With the definition of truth at a world, we can define an entailment relation between formulas. A formula B entails formula A if and only if, whenever formula B is true, formula A is true as well. Here, “whenever” means both “whichever model we consider” as well as “whichever world in that model we consider.” Definition. If Gamma is a set of formulas and formula A a formula, then Gamma entails formula A , in symbols: Gamma entails A , if and only if for every model model M is the ordered triple W, R, V and world w belongs to W , if model M satisfies B at world w for every B belongs to Gamma , then model M satisfies A at world w . If Gamma contains a single formula B , then we write B entails A . End definition. Example. To show that a formula entails another, we have to reason about all models, using the definition of satisfaction in model M at world w . For instance, to show if p then possibly p entails if necessarily not p then not p , we might argue as follows: Consider a model model M is the ordered triple W, R, V and w belongs to W , and suppose model M satisfies the conditional from p to possibly p at world w . Then model M satisfies necessarily not p at world w and model M does not satisfy not p at world w . Since model M does not satisfy not p at world w , model M satisfies p at world w . By assumption, model M satisfies the conditional from p to possibly p at world w , hence model M satisfies possibility of p at world w .
