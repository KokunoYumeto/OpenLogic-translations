Introduction

The completeness theorem is one of the most fundamental results about logic. It comes in two formulations, the equivalence of which we'll prove. In its first formulation it says something fundamental about the relationship between semantic consequence and our derivation system: if a sentence A follows from some sentences Gamma, then there is also a derivation that establishes Gamma syntactically derives A. Thus, the derivation system is as strong as it can possibly be without proving things that don't actually follow.

In its second formulation, it can be stated as a model existence result: every consistent set of sentences is satisfiable. Consistency is a proof-theoretic notion: it says that our derivation system is unable to produce certain derivations. But who's to say that just because there are no derivations of a certain sort from Gamma, it's guaranteed that there is a valuation v with valuation v satisfies Gamma? Before the completeness theorem was first proved—in fact before we had the derivation systems we now do—the great German mathematician David Hilbert held the view that consistency of mathematical theories guarantees the existence of the objects they are about. He put it as follows in a letter to Gottlob Frege:

If the arbitrarily given axioms do not contradict one another with all their consequences, then they are true and the things defined by the axioms exist. This is for me the criterion of truth and existence.

Frege vehemently disagreed. The second formulation of the completeness theorem shows that Hilbert was right in at least the sense that if the axioms are consistent, then some valuation exists that makes them all true.

These aren't the only reasons the completeness theorem—or rather, its proof—is important. It has a number of important consequences, some of which we'll discuss separately. For instance, since any derivation that shows Gamma syntactically derives A is finite and so can only use finitely many of the sentences in Gamma, it follows by the completeness theorem that if A is a consequence of Gamma, it is already a consequence of a finite subset of Gamma. This is called compactness. Equivalently, if every finite subset of Gamma is consistent, then Gamma itself must be consistent.

Although the compactness theorem follows from the completeness theorem via the detour through derivations, it is also possible to use the the proof of the completeness theorem to establish it directly. For what the proof does is take a set of sentences with a certain property—consistency—and constructs a structure out of this set that has certain properties (in this case, that it satisfies the set). Almost the very same construction can be used to directly establish compactness, by starting from “finitely satisfiable” sets of sentences instead of consistent ones.

Outline of the Proof

The proof of the completeness theorem is a bit complex, and upon first reading it, it is easy to get lost. So let us outline the proof. The first step is a shift of perspective, that allows us to see a route to a proof. When completeness is thought of as “whenever Gamma semantically entails A then Gamma syntactically derives A,” it may be hard to even come up with an idea: for to show that Gamma syntactically derives A we have to find a derivation, and it does not look like the hypothesis that Gamma semantically entails A helps us for this in any way. For some proof systems it is possible to directly construct a derivation, but we will take a slightly different approach. The shift in perspective required is this: completeness can also be formulated as: “if Gamma is consistent, it is satisfiable.” Perhaps we can use the information in Gamma together with the hypothesis that it is consistent to construct a valuation that satisfies every formula in Gamma. After all, we know what kind of valuation we are looking for: one that is as Gamma describes it!

If Gamma contains only propositional variables, it is easy to construct a model for it. All we have to do is come up with

a valuation v such that valuation v satisfies p for all p in Gamma. Well, let valuation v assigns verum to p iff p belongs to Gamma.

Now suppose Gamma contains some formula not B, with B atomic. We might worry that the construction of valuation v interferes with the possibility of making not B true. But here's where the consistency of Gamma comes in: if not B belongs to Gamma, then B does not belong to Gamma, or else Gamma would be inconsistent. And if B does not belong to Gamma, then according to our construction of valuation v, valuation v does not satisfy B, so valuation v satisfies not B. So far so good.

What if Gamma contains complex, non-atomic formulas? Say it contains A and B. To make that true, we should proceed as if both A and B were in Gamma. And if the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma, then we will have to make at least one of them true, i.e., proceed as if one of them was in Gamma.

This suggests the following idea: we add additional formulas to Gamma so as to (a) keep the resulting set consistent and (b) make sure that for every possible atomic sentence A, either A is in the resulting set, or not A is, and (c) such that, whenever A and B is in the set, so are both A and B, if A or B is in the set, at least one of A or B is also, etc. We keep doing this (potentially forever). Call the set of all formulas so added Gamma star. Then our construction above would provide us with a valuation v for which we could prove, by induction, that it satisfies all sentences in Gamma star, and hence also all sentence in Gamma since Gamma is a subset of Gamma star. It turns out that guaranteeing (a) and (b) is enough. A set of sentences for which (b) holds is called complete. So our task will be to extend the consistent set Gamma to a consistent and complete set Gamma star.

So here's what we'll do. First we investigate the properties of complete consistent sets, in particular we prove that a complete consistent set contains A and B iff it contains both A and B, A or B iff it contains at least one of them, etc. (the proposition about complete consistent sets). We'll then take the consistent set Gamma and show that it can be extended to a consistent and complete set Gamma star (Lindenbaum's Lemma). This set Gamma star is what we'll use to define our valuation v of Gamma star. The valuation is determined by the propositional variables in Gamma star (the canonical valuation definition). We'll use the properties of complete consistent sets to show that indeed valuation v of Gamma star satisfies A iff A belongs to Gamma star (the Truth Lemma), and thus in particular, valuation v of Gamma star satisfies every formula in Gamma.

Complete Consistent Sets of sentence

Definition: Complete set

A set Gamma of sentences is complete iff for any sentence A, either A belongs to Gamma or not A belongs to Gamma.

Explanation

Complete sets of sentences leave no questions unanswered. For any sentence A, Gamma “says” if A is true or false. The importance of complete sets extends beyond the proof of the completeness theorem. A theory which is complete and axiomatizable, for instance, is always decidable.

Explanation

Complete consistent sets are important in the completeness proof since we can guarantee that every consistent set of sentences Gamma is contained in a complete consistent set Gamma star. A complete consistent set contains, for each sentence A, either A or its negation not A, but not both. This is true in particular for propositional variables, so from a complete consistent set, we can construct a valuation where the truth value assigned to propositional variables is defined according to which propositional variables are in Gamma star. This valuation can then be shown to make all sentences in Gamma star (and hence also all those in Gamma) true. The proof of this latter fact requires that not A belongs to Gamma star iff A does not belong to Gamma star, the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma star iff A belongs to Gamma star or B belongs to Gamma star, etc.

In what follows, we will often tacitly use the properties of reflexivity, monotonicity, and transitivity of the syntactic derivability relation (see

the proof-theoretic notions section for sequent calculus, the proof-theoretic notions section for natural deduction, the proof-theoretic notions section for axiomatic derivation, the proof-theoretic notions section for tableaux).

Proposition: Properties of complete consistent sets

Suppose Gamma is complete and consistent. Then:

  1. If Gamma syntactically derives A, then A belongs to Gamma.

    the conjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma iff both A belongs to Gamma and B belongs to Gamma.

    the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma iff either A belongs to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma.

    the conditional from A to B belongs to Gamma iff either A does not belong to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma.

Proof

Let us suppose for all of the following that Gamma is complete and consistent.

  1. If Gamma syntactically derives A, then A belongs to Gamma.

    Suppose that Gamma syntactically derives A. Suppose to the contrary that A does not belong to Gamma. Since Gamma is complete, not A belongs to Gamma. By

    the sequent calculus proposition that derivability plus an explicit negation yields inconsistency, the natural deduction proposition that derivability plus an explicit negation yields inconsistency, the axiomatic derivation proposition that derivability plus an explicit negation yields inconsistency, the tableaux proposition that derivability plus an explicit negation yields inconsistency, Gamma is inconsistent. This contradicts the assumption that Gamma is consistent. Hence, it cannot be the case that A does not belong to Gamma, so A belongs to Gamma.

    Exercise.

    First we show that if the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma, then either A belongs to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma. Suppose the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma but A does not belong to Gamma and B does not belong to Gamma. Since Gamma is complete, not A belongs to Gamma and not B belongs to Gamma. By

    the sequent calculus proposition about disjunction and derivability, the natural deduction proposition about disjunction and derivability, the axiomatic derivation proposition about disjunction and derivability, the tableaux proposition about disjunction and derivability, item (1), Gamma is inconsistent, a contradiction. Hence, either A belongs to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma.

    For the reverse direction, suppose that A belongs to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma. By

    the sequent calculus proposition about disjunction and derivability, the natural deduction proposition about disjunction and derivability, the axiomatic derivation proposition about disjunction and derivability, the tableaux proposition about disjunction and derivability, item (2), Gamma syntactically derives the disjunction of A and B. By the complete-consistent-set closure-under-derivability proposition, the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma, as required.

Lindenbaum's Lemma

Explanation

We now prove a lemma that shows that any consistent set of sentences is contained in some set of sentences which is not just consistent, but also complete. The proof works by adding one sentence at a time, guaranteeing at each step that the set remains consistent. We do this so that for every A, either A or not A gets added at some stage. The union of all stages in that construction then contains either A or its negation not A and is thus complete. It is also consistent, since we make sure at each stage not to introduce an inconsistency.

Lemma: Lindenbaum's Lemma

Every consistent set Gamma in a language L can be extended to a complete and consistent set Gamma star.

Proof

Let Gamma be consistent. Let A sub zero, A sub one, and so on be an enumeration of all the sentences of language L. Define Gamma sub zero equals Gamma, and Gamma sub n plus one equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing A sub n if that union is consistent; otherwise Gamma sub n plus one equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing not A sub n Let Gamma star equals the union of all Gamma sub n for n greater than or equal to zero.

Each Gamma sub n is consistent: Gamma sub zero is consistent by definition. If Gamma sub n plus one equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing A sub n, this is because the latter is consistent. If it isn't, Gamma sub n plus one equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing not A sub n. We have to verify that Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing not A sub n is consistent. Suppose it's not. Then both Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing A sub n and Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing not A sub n are inconsistent. This means that Gamma sub n would be inconsistent by

the axiomatic derivation proposition that two inconsistent one-formula extensions make the original set inconsistent, the sequent calculus proposition that two inconsistent one-formula extensions make the original set inconsistent, the natural deduction proposition that two inconsistent one-formula extensions make the original set inconsistent, the tableaux proposition that two inconsistent one-formula extensions make the original set inconsistent, contrary to the induction hypothesis.

For every n and every i less than n, Gamma sub i is a subset of Gamma sub n. This follows by a simple induction on n. For n equals zero, there are no indices i less than zero, so the claim holds automatically. For the inductive step, suppose it is true for n. We show that if i is less than n plus one then Gamma sub i is a subset of Gamma sub n plus one. We have Gamma sub n plus one equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing A sub n or equals Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing not A sub n by construction. So Gamma sub n is a subset of Gamma sub n plus one. If i is less than n plus one, then Gamma sub i is a subset of Gamma sub n by inductive hypothesis (if i is less than n) or the trivial fact that Gamma sub n is a subset of Gamma sub n (if i equals n). We get that Gamma sub i is a subset of Gamma sub n plus one by transitivity of the subset relation.

From this it follows that Gamma star is consistent. Here's why: Let Gamma prime, a subset of Gamma star, be finite. Each member B of Gamma prime is also in Gamma sub i for some i. Let n be the largest of these. Since Gamma sub i is a subset of Gamma sub n if i is less than or equal to n, every member B of Gamma prime is also in Gamma sub n, i.e., Gamma prime is a subset of Gamma sub n, and Gamma sub n is consistent. So, every finite subset Gamma prime contained in Gamma star is consistent. By the proof-compactness proposition for axiomatic derivation, the proof-compactness proposition for sequent calculus, the proof-compactness proposition for natural deduction, the proof-compactness proposition for tableaux, Gamma star is consistent.

Every sentence of language L appears on the list used to define Gamma star. If A sub n does not belong to Gamma star, then that is because Gamma sub n union the singleton set containing A sub n was inconsistent. But then not A sub n belongs to Gamma star, so Gamma star is complete.

End of proof.

Construction of a Model

Explanation

We are now ready to define a valuation that makes all A in Gamma true. To do this, we first apply Lindenbaum's Lemma: we get a complete consistent set Gamma star containing Gamma. We let the propositional variables in Gamma star determine valuation v of Gamma star.

Definition: Canonical valuation from Gamma star

Suppose Gamma star is a complete consistent set of formulas. Then we let valuation v of Gamma star assigns verum to p when p belongs to Gamma star, and assigns falsum to p otherwise

Lemma: Truth Lemma for the canonical valuation

valuation v of Gamma star satisfies A iff A belongs to Gamma star.

Proof

We prove both directions simultaneously, and by induction on A.

Case: valuation v of Gamma star does not satisfy falsum by definition of satisfaction. On the other hand, falsum does not belong to Gamma star since Gamma star is consistent.

Case: valuation v of Gamma star satisfies propositional variable p iff valuation v of Gamma star assigns verum to p (by the definition of satisfaction) iff p belongs to Gamma star (by the construction of valuation v of Gamma star).

Case: valuation v of Gamma star satisfies the current induction formula iff valuation v of Gamma star does not satisfy B (by definition of satisfaction). By induction hypothesis, valuation v of Gamma star does not satisfy B iff B does not belong to Gamma star. Since Gamma star is consistent and complete, B does not belong to Gamma star iff not B belongs to Gamma star.

Case: valuation v of Gamma star satisfies the current induction formula iff valuation v of Gamma star satisfies B or valuation v of Gamma star satisfies C (by definition of satisfaction) iff B belongs to Gamma star or C belongs to Gamma star (by induction hypothesis). This is the case iff the disjunction of B and C belongs to Gamma star (by the proposition about complete consistent setsthe complete-consistent-set disjunction proposition).

    End of proof.

    The Completeness Theorem

    Explanation

    Let's combine our results: we arrive at the completeness theorem.

    Theorem: Model-existence form of completeness

    Let Gamma be a set of sentences. If Gamma is consistent, it is satisfiable.

    Proof

    Suppose Gamma is consistent. By Lindenbaum's Lemma, there is a set Gamma star containing Gamma which is consistent and complete. By the Truth Lemma, valuation v of Gamma star satisfies A iff A belongs to Gamma star. From this it follows in particular that for all A in Gamma, valuation v of Gamma star satisfies A, so Gamma is satisfiable.

    End of proof.

    Corollary: Semantic-consequence form of completeness

    For all Gamma and sentences A: if Gamma semantically entails A then Gamma syntactically derives A.

    Proof

    Note that the Gamma's in the semantic-consequence corollary to the Completeness Theorem and the model-existence Completeness Theorem are universally quantified. To make sure we do not confuse ourselves, let us restate the model-existence Completeness Theorem using a different variable: for any set of sentences Delta, if Delta is consistent, it is satisfiable. By contraposition, if Delta is not satisfiable, then Delta is inconsistent. We will use this to prove the corollary.

    Suppose that Gamma semantically entails A. Then Gamma union the singleton set containing not A is unsatisfiable by the proposition relating entailment to unsatisfiability. Taking Gamma union the singleton set containing not A as our Delta, the previous version of the model-existence Completeness Theorem gives us that Gamma union the singleton set containing not A is inconsistent. By

    the axiomatic derivation proposition equating derivability of A with inconsistency after adjoining not A, the sequent calculus proposition equating derivability of A with inconsistency after adjoining not A, the natural deduction proposition equating derivability of A with inconsistency after adjoining not A, the tableaux proposition equating derivability of A with inconsistency after adjoining not A, Gamma syntactically derives A.

    End of proof.

    The Compactness Theorem

    One important consequence of the completeness theorem is the compactness theorem. The compactness theorem states that if each finite subset of a set of sentences is satisfiable, the entire set is satisfiable—even if the set itself is infinite. This is far from obvious. There is nothing that seems to rule out, at first glance at least, the possibility of there being infinite sets of sentences which are contradictory, but the contradiction only arises, so to speak, from the infinite number. The compactness theorem says that such a scenario can be ruled out: there are no unsatisfiable infinite sets of sentences each finite subset of which is satisfiable. Like the completeness theorem, it has a version related to entailment: if an infinite set of sentences entails something, already a finite subset does.

    Definition: Finitely satisfiable set

    A set Gamma of formulas is finitely satisfiable iff every finite subset Gamma sub zero of Gamma is satisfiable.

    Theorem: Compactness in two forms

    The following hold for any sentences Gamma and A:

    1. Gamma semantically entails A iff there is a finite subset Gamma sub zero of Gamma such that Gamma sub zero semantically entails A.

    2. Gamma is satisfiable iff it is finitely satisfiable.

    Proof

    We prove (2). If Gamma is satisfiable, then there is a valuation v such that valuation v satisfies A for all A in Gamma. Of course, this valuation v also satisfies every finite subset of Gamma, so Gamma is finitely satisfiable.

    Now suppose that Gamma is finitely satisfiable. Then every finite subset Gamma sub zero of Gamma is satisfiable. By soundness ( the axiomatic derivation soundness corollary that satisfiability implies consistency, the sequent calculus soundness corollary that satisfiability implies consistency, the natural deduction soundness corollary that satisfiability implies consistency, the tableaux soundness corollary that satisfiability implies consistency), every finite subset is consistent. Then Gamma itself must be consistent by

    the proof-compactness proposition for axiomatic derivation, the proof-compactness proposition for sequent calculus, the proof-compactness proposition for natural deduction, the proof-compactness proposition for tableaux. By completeness (the model-existence Completeness Theorem), since Gamma is consistent, it is satisfiable.

    End of proof.

    A Direct Proof of the Compactness Theorem

    We can prove the Compactness Theorem directly, without appealing to the Completeness Theorem, using the same ideas as in the proof of the completeness theorem. In the proof of the Completeness Theorem we started with a consistent set Gamma of sentences, expanded it to a consistent and complete set Gamma star of sentences, and then showed that in the valuation v of Gamma star constructed from Gamma star, all sentences of Gamma are true, so Gamma is satisfiable.

    We can use the same method to show that a finitely satisfiable set of sentences is satisfiable. We just have to prove the corresponding versions of the results leading to the truth lemma where we replace “consistent” with “finitely satisfiable.”

    Proposition: Complete finitely satisfiable sets

    Suppose Gamma is complete and finitely satisfiable. Then:

    the conjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma iff both A belongs to Gamma and B belongs to Gamma.

    the disjunction of A and B belongs to Gamma iff either A belongs to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma.

    the conditional from A to B belongs to Gamma iff either A does not belong to Gamma or B belongs to Gamma.
