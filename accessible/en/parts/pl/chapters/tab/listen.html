Rules and tableau

A tableau is a systematic survey of the possible ways a sentence can be true or false in a structure. The building blocks of a tableau are signed formulas: sentences plus a truth value “sign,” either true or false. These signed formulas are arranged in a (downward growing) tree.

Definition of a signed formula

A signed formula is a pair consisting of a truth value and a sentence, i.e., either: the complete formula A, carrying the true sign or the complete formula A, carrying the false sign period

Intuitively, we might read the complete formula A, carrying the true sign as “A might be true” and the complete formula A, carrying the false sign as “A might be false” (in some structure).

Each signed formula in the tree is either an assumption (which are listed at the very top of the tree), or it is obtained from a signed formula above it by one of a number of rules of inference. There are two rules for each possible main operator of the preceding formula, one for the case where the sign is true, and one for the case where the sign is false. Some rules allow the tree to branch, and some only add signed formulas to the branch. A rule may be (and often must be) applied not to the immediately preceding signed formula, but to any signed formula in the branch from the root to the place the rule is applied.

A branch is closed when it contains both the complete formula A, carrying the true sign and the complete formula A, carrying the false sign. A closed tableau is one where every branch is closed. Under the intuitive interpretation, any branch describes a joint possibility, but the complete formula A, carrying the true sign and the complete formula A, carrying the false sign are not jointly possible. In other words, if a branch is closed, the possibility it describes has been ruled out. In particular, that means that a closed tableau rules out all possibilities of simultaneously making every assumption of the form the complete formula A, carrying the true sign true and every assumption of the form the complete formula A, carrying the false sign false.

A closed tableau for A is a closed tableau with root the complete formula A, carrying the false sign. If such a closed tableau exists, all possibilities for A being false have been ruled out; i.e., A must be true in every structure.

            The cut tableau rule is not applied “to” a previous signed formula; rather, it allows every branch in a tableau to be split in two, one branch containing the complete formula A, carrying the true sign, the other the complete formula A, carrying the false sign. It is not necessary—any set of signed formulas with a closed tableau has one not using cut tableau rule—but it allows us to combine tableaus in a convenient way.

            tableau

            Explanation

            We've said what an assumption is, and we've given the rules of inference. Tableaus are inductively generated from these: each tableau either is a single branch consisting of one or more assumptions, or it results from a tableau by applying one of the rules of inference on a branch.

            Definition of a tableau derivation

            A tableau for assumptions the complete formula A sub one, carrying sign S sub one, and so on, the complete formula A sub n, carrying sign S sub n (where each S sub i is either true or false) is a finite tree of signed formulas satisfying the following conditions:

            1. The n topmost signed formulas of the tree are the complete formula A sub i, carrying sign S sub i, one below the other.

            2. Every signed formula in the tree that is not one of the assumptions results from a correct application of an inference rule to a signed formula in the branch above it.

            A branch of a tableau is closed iff it contains both the complete formula A, carrying the true sign and the complete formula A, carrying the false sign, and open otherwise. A tableau in which every branch is closed is a closed tableau (for its set of assumptions). If a tableau is not closed, i.e., if it contains at least one open branch, it is open.

            Example of extending a tableau derivation

                                                                  Soundness

                                                                  Explanation

                                                                  A derivation system, such as tableaux, is sound if it cannot derive things that do not actually hold. Soundness is thus a kind of guaranteed safety property for derivation systems. Depending on which proof theoretic property is in question, we would like to know for instance, that

                                                                  1. every derivable A is a tautology;

                                                                  2. if a sentence is derivable from some others, it is also a consequence of them;

                                                                  3. if a set of sentences is inconsistent, it is unsatisfiable.

                                                                  These are important properties of a derivation system. If any of them do not hold, the derivation system is deficient—it would derive too much. Consequently, establishing the soundness of a derivation system is of the utmost importance.

                                                                  Because all these proof-theoretic properties are defined via closed tableaus of some kind or other, proving (1)–(3) above requires proving something about the semantic properties of closed tableaus. We will first define what it means for a signed formula to be satisfied in a structure, and then show that if a tableau is closed, no structure satisfies all its assumptions. (1)–(3) then follow as corollaries from this result.

                                                                  Definition of satisfaction for signed formulas and branches

                                                                  A valuation v satisfies a signed formula A carrying the true sign iff valuation v satisfies A, and it satisfies the complete formula A, carrying the false sign iff valuation v does not satisfy A. valuation v satisfies a set of signed formulas Gamma iff it satisfies every signed formula A with sign S that belongs to Gamma. Gamma is satisfiable if there is a valuation that satisfies it, and unsatisfiable otherwise.

                                                                  Theorem: a closed tableau has no satisfying valuation

                                                                  If Gamma has a closed tableau, Gamma is unsatisfiable.

                                                                  Proof

                                                                  Let's call a branch of a tableau satisfiable iff the set of signed formulas on it is satisfiable, and let's call a tableau satisfiable if it contains at least one satisfiable branch.

                                                                  We show the following: Extending a satisfiable tableau by one of the rules of inference always results in a satisfiable tableau. This will prove the theorem: any closed tableau results by applying rules of inference to the tableau consisting only of assumptions from Gamma. So if Gamma were satisfiable, any tableau for it would be satisfiable. A closed tableau, however, is clearly not satisfiable: every branch contains both the complete formula A, carrying the true sign and the complete formula A, carrying the false sign, and no structure can both satisfy and not satisfy A.

                                                                  Suppose we have a satisfiable tableau, i.e., a tableau with at least one satisfiable branch. Applying a rule of inference either adds signed formulas to a branch, or splits a branch in two. If the tableau has a satisfiable branch which is not extended by the rule application in question, it remains a satisfiable branch in the extended tableau, so the extended tableau is satisfiable. So we only have to consider the case where a rule is applied to a satisfiable branch.

                                                                  Let Gamma be the set of signed formulas on that branch, and let A with sign S, belonging to Gamma, be the signed formula to which the rule is applied. If the rule does not result in a split branch, we have to show that the extended branch, i.e., Gamma together with the conclusions of the rule, is still satisfiable. If the rule results in a split branch, we have to show that at least one of the two resulting branches is satisfiable.

                                                                  First, we consider the possible inferences that do not result in a split branch.

                                                                  1. The branch is expanded by applying true-negation tableau rule to the member of Gamma consisting of not B with the true sign. Then the extended branch contains the signed formulas Gamma union the set containing the complete formula B, carrying the false sign. Suppose valuation v satisfies every formula in Gamma. In particular, valuation v satisfies not B. Thus, valuation v does not satisfy B, i.e., valuation v satisfies the complete formula B, carrying the false sign.

                                                                  3. Suppose valuation v satisfies every formula in Gamma, in particular valuation v satisfies the complete conjunction B and C. Then valuation v satisfies B and valuation v satisfies C. This means that valuation v satisfies both the complete formula B, carrying the true sign and the complete formula C, carrying the true sign.

                                                                  4. The branch is expanded by applying false-disjunction tableau rule to the member of Gamma consisting of the disjunction B or C with the false sign: Exercise.

                                                                  5. The branch is expanded by applying false-conditional tableau rule to the member of Gamma consisting of the conditional from B to C with the false sign: This results in two new signed formulas on the branch: the complete formula B, carrying the true sign and the complete formula C, carrying the false sign. Suppose valuation v satisfies every formula in Gamma, in particular valuation v does not satisfy the complete conditional from B to C. Then valuation v satisfies B and valuation v does not satisfy C. This means that valuation v satisfies both the complete formula B, carrying the true sign and the complete formula C, carrying the false sign.

                                                                  1. The branch is expanded by applying false-conjunction tableau rule to the member of Gamma consisting of the conjunction B and C with the false sign, which results in two branches, a left one continuing through the complete formula B, carrying the false sign and a right one through the complete formula C, carrying the false sign. Suppose valuation v satisfies every formula in Gamma, in particular valuation v does not satisfy the complete conjunction B and C. Then valuation v does not satisfy B or valuation v does not satisfy C. In the former case, valuation v satisfies the complete formula B, carrying the false sign, i.e., valuation v satisfies the formulas on the left branch. In the latter, valuation v satisfies the complete formula C, carrying the false sign, i.e., valuation v satisfies the formulas on the right branch.
