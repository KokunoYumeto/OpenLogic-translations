Editorial note

The material in this chapter presents the construction of the number systems in naïve set theory. It is taken from Tim Button's Open Set Theory text.

From the natural numbers to the integers

Here are two basic realisations:

  1. Every integer can be written in the form n minus m, with natural numbers n and m.

  2. The information encoded in an expression n minus m can equally be encoded by an ordered pair n comma m.

We already know that the ordered pairs of natural numbers are the elements of the Cartesian square of the natural numbers. And we are assuming that we understand the natural numbers. So here is a naïve suggestion, based on the two realisations we have had: let's treat integers as ordered pairs of natural numbers.

In fact, this suggestion is too naïve. Obviously we want it to be the case that zero minus two equals four minus six. But evidently the ordered pair zero comma two is not equal to the ordered pair four comma six. So we cannot simply say that the Cartesian square of the natural numbers is the set of integers.

Generalising from the preceding problem, what we want is the following: a minus b equals c minus d if and only if a plus d equals c plus b (It should be obvious that this is how integers are meant to behave: just add b and d to both sides.) And the easy way to guarantee this behaviour is just to define an equivalence relation between ordered pairs, the integer-equivalence relation, as follows: the ordered pair a comma b is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair c comma d if and only if a plus d equals c plus b We now have to show that this is an equivalence relation.

Integer equivalence is an equivalence relation

the integer-equivalence relation is an equivalence relation.

Proof

We must show that the integer-equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

Reflexivity: Evidently the ordered pair a comma b is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair a comma b, since a plus b equals b plus a.

Symmetry: Suppose the ordered pair a comma b is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair c comma d, so a plus d equals c plus b. Then c plus b equals a plus d, so that the ordered pair c comma d is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair a comma b.

Transitivity: Suppose the ordered pair a comma b is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair c comma d, and that pair is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair m comma n. So a plus d equals c plus b and c plus n equals m plus d. So a plus d plus c plus n equals c plus b plus m plus d, and so a plus n equals m plus b. Hence the ordered pair a comma b is integer-equivalent to the ordered pair m comma n.

End of proof.

Now we can use this equivalence relation to take equivalence classes:

Definition of the integers as equivalence classes

The integers are the equivalence classes, under integer equivalence, of ordered pairs of natural numbers; that is, the set of integers is the quotient of the natural-number pairs by integer equivalence.

Now, one might have plenty of different philosophical reactions to this stipulative definition. Before we consider those reactions, though, it is worth continuing with some of the technicalities.

Having said what the integers are, we shall need to define basic functions and relations on them. Let's write the integer-equivalence class of m comma n for the equivalence class under integer equivalence with the ordered pair m comma n as an element.Note: using the notation introduced in the definition of an equivalence class, we would have written the integer-equivalence class of the ordered pair m comma n for the same thing. But that's just a bit harder to read. That is: the integer-equivalence class of m comma n equals the set of natural-number pairs a comma b that are integer-equivalent to m comma n So now we offer some definitions: three definitions on integer-equivalence classes. Their sum is the class with first coordinate a plus c and second coordinate b plus d. Their product is the class with first coordinate a times c plus b times d and second coordinate a times d plus b times c. The class of a comma b is at most the class of c comma d if and only if a plus d is at most b plus c (As is common, I'm using `a times b' to stand for `a times b', just to make the axioms easier to read.) Now, we need to make sure that these definitions behave as they ought to. Spelling out what this means, and checking it through, is rather laborious; we relegate the details to the Ordered Rings and Fields section. But the short point is: everything works!

One final thing remains. We have constructed the integers using natural numbers. But this will mean that the natural numbers are not themselves integers. We will return to the philosophical significance of this in the Some Philosophical Reflections section. On a purely technical front, though, we will need some way to be able to treat natural numbers as integers. The idea is quite easy: for each natural number n, we just stipulate that the integer embedding of n equals the integer-equivalence class of n comma zero. We need to confirm that this definition is well-behaved, i.e., that for any natural numbers m and n three embedding laws: the integer embedding of m plus n equals the sum of the integer embeddings of m and n; the integer embedding of m times n equals their embedded product; and m is at most n if and only if embedded m is at most embedded n But this is all pretty straightforward. For example, to show that the second of these obtains, we can simply help ourselves to the behaviour of the natural numbers and reason as follows:

four-line calculation for multiplication under the integer embedding. The integer embedding of m times n is the class with coordinates m times n and zero; by the class-product definition this is the class with first coordinate m times n plus zero times zero and second coordinate m times zero plus zero times n; this is the product of the classes of m comma zero and n comma zero; therefore it equals embedded m times embedded n

We leave it as an exercise to confirm that the other two conditions hold.

From the integers to the rational numbers

We just saw how to construct the integers from the natural numbers, using some naïve set theory. We shall now see how to construct the rationals from the integers in a very similar way. Our initial realisations are:

  1. Every rational can be written in the form the fraction with numerator i and denominator j, where both i and j are integers but j is non-zero.

  2. The information encoded in an expression the fraction with numerator i and denominator j can equally be encoded in an ordered pair i comma j.

The obvious approach would be to think of the rationals as ordered pairs drawn from the Cartesian product of the integers with the nonzero integers. As before, though, that would be a bit too naïve, since we want the fraction with numerator three and denominator two equals the fraction with numerator six and denominator four, but the ordered pair three comma two is not equal to the ordered pair six comma four. More generally, we will want the following: a over b equals c over d if and only if a times d equals b times c To get this, we define an equivalence relation on the Cartesian product of the integers with the nonzero integers thus: the ordered pair a comma b is rational-equivalent to the ordered pair c comma d if and only if a times d equals b times c We must check that this is an equivalence relation. This is very much like the case of integer equivalence, and we will leave it as an exercise.

But it allows us to say:

Definition of the rationals as equivalence classes

The rationals are the equivalence classes, under rational equivalence, of pairs of integers (whose second element is non-zero). That is, the set of rational numbers is the quotient of integer pairs with nonzero second coordinate by rational equivalence.

As with the integers, we also want to define some basic operations. Where the equivalence class of i comma j modulo rational equivalence is the equivalence class under rational equivalence with the ordered pair i comma j as an element, we say: definitions of rational-class arithmetic and order. The sum of the classes a comma b and c comma d is the class with numerator a times d plus b times c and denominator b times d. Their product is the class with numerator a times c and denominator b times d. For order, the first class is at most the second exactly when their difference is represented by a nonnegative integer numerator i and positive integer denominator j for some natural number i and nonzero natural number j.

We then need to check that these definitions behave as they ought to; and we relegate this to the Ordered Rings and Fields section. But they indeed do! Finally, we want some way to treat integers as rationals; so for each integer i, we stipulate that the rational embedding of integer i equals the rational-equivalence class of i comma integer one. Again, we check that all of this behaves correctly in the Ordered Rings and Fields section.

The Real Line

The next step is to show how to construct the reals from the rationals. Before that, we need to understand what is distinctive about the reals.

The reals behave very much like the rationals. (Technically, both are examples of ordered fields; for the definition of this, see the definition of an ordered field.) Now, if you worked through the exercises to the Size of Sets chapter, you will know that there are strictly more reals than rationals, i.e., that the cardinality of the rational numbers is strictly less than the cardinality of the real numbers. This was first proved by Cantor. But it's been known for about two and a half millennia that there are irrational numbers, i.e., reals which are not rational. Indeed:

The square root of two is irrational

the square root of two is not rational, i.e., the square root of two is not an element of the rational numbers

Proof

Suppose, for reductio, that the square root of two is rational. So the square root of two equals the fraction with numerator m and denominator n for some natural numbers m and n. Indeed, we can choose m and n so that the fraction cannot be reduced any further. Re-organising, m squared equals two times n squared. From here, we can complete the proof in two ways:

First, geometrically (following Tennenbaum).This proof is reported by John Conway (two thousand six). Consider these squares:

Since m squared equals two times n squared, the region where the two squares of side n overlap has the same area as the region which neither of the two squares cover; i.e., the area of the orange square equals the sum of the area of the two unshaded squares. So where the orange square has side p, and each unshaded square has side q, p squared equals two times q squared. But now the square root of two equals the fraction with numerator p and denominator q, with p less than m and q less than n and p and q in the natural numbers. This contradicts the fact that m and n were chosen to be as small as possible.

Second, formally. Since m squared equals two times n squared, it follows that m is even. (It is easy to show that, if x is odd, then x to the power two is odd.) So m equals two times r, for some natural number r. Rearranging, two times r squared equals n squared,

so n is also even. So both m and n are even, and hence the fraction with numerator m and denominator n can be reduced further. Contradiction!

End of proof.

In passing, this diagrammatic proof allows us to revisit the material from the More Myth than History section. Tennenbaum (1927–2006) was a thoroughly modern mathematician; but the proof is undeniably lovely, completely rigorous, and appeals to geometric intuition!

In any case: the reals are “more expansive” than the rationals. In some sense, there are “gaps” in the rationals, and these are filled by the reals. Weierstrass realised that this describes a single property of the real numbers, which distinguishes them from the rationals, namely the Completeness Property: Every non-empty set of real numbers with an upper bound has a least upper bound.

It is easy to see that the rationals do not have the Completeness Property. For example, consider the set of rationals less than the square root of two, i.e.: the set of rational p such that p squared is less than two or p is negative This has an upper bound in the rationals; its elements are all smaller than three, for example. But what is its least upper bound? We want to say `the square root of two'; but we have just seen that the square root of two is not rational. And there is no least rational number greater than the square root of two. So the set has an upper bound but no least upper bound. Hence the rationals lack the Completeness Property.

By contrast, the continuum “morally ought” to have the Completeness Property. We do not just want the square root of two to be a real number; we want to fill all the “gaps” in the rational line. Indeed, we want the continuum itself to have no “gaps” in it. That is just what we will get via Completeness.

From the rational numbers to the real numbers

In essence, the Completeness Property shows that any point alpha of the real line divides that line into two halves perfectly: those for which alpha is the least upper bound, and those for which alpha is the greatest lower bound. To construct the real numbers from the rational numbers, Dedekind suggested that we simply think of the reals as the cuts that partition the rationals. That is, we identify the square root of two with the cut which separates the rationals less than the square root of two from the rationals greater than the square root of two.

Let's tidy this up. If we cut the rational numbers into two halves, we can uniquely identify the partition we made just by considering its bottom half. So, getting precise, we offer the following definition:

Definition of a Dedekind cut

A cut alpha is any non-empty proper initial segment of the rationals with no greatest element. That is, alpha is a cut iff:

  1. non-empty, proper: alpha is a nonempty proper subset of the rational numbers

  2. initial: for all rational numbers p and q: if p is less than q, and q is in alpha then p is an element of alpha

  3. no maximum: for all p in alpha there is a q in alpha such that p is less than q

Then the set of real numbers is the set of cuts.

So now we can say that the square root of two equals the cut of all rational p such that p squared is less than two or p is negative. Of course, we need to check that this is a cut, but we relegate that to the Ordered Rings and Fields section.

As before, having defined some entities, we next need to define basic functions and relations upon them. We begin with an easy one: alpha is at most beta if and only if alpha is a subset of beta This definition of an order allows to state the central result, that the set of cuts has the Completeness Property. Spelled out fully, the statement has this shape. If S is a non-empty set of cuts with an upper bound, then S has a least upper bound. In more detail: there is a cut, lambda, which is an upper bound for S, i.e. for every alpha in S, alpha is a subset of lambda, and lambda is the least such cut, i.e. for every real cut beta, if every alpha in S is a subset of beta, then lambda is a subset of beta. Now here is the proof of the result:

Completeness of the set of cuts

The set of cuts has the Completeness Property.

Proof

Let S be any non-empty set of cuts with an upper bound. Let lambda be the union of S.

We first claim that lambda is a cut:

  1. Since S is nonempty, at least one cut is in S, so the empty set is not equal to lambda. Since S is a set of cuts, lambda is a subset of the rational numbers. Since S has an upper bound, some rational p is absent from every cut alpha in S. So p is not an element of lambda, and hence lambda is a proper subset of the rational numbers.

  2. Suppose p is less than q, and q belongs to lambda. So there is some alpha in S such that q is an element of alpha. Since alpha is a cut, p is an element of alpha. So p is an element of lambda.

  3. Suppose p is an element of lambda. So there is some alpha in S such that p is an element of alpha. Since alpha is a cut, there is some q in alpha such that p is less than q. So q is an element of lambda.

This proves the claim. Moreover, clearly for every alpha in S, alpha is a subset of the union of S, which equals lambda, i.e. lambda is an upper bound on S. So now suppose the real cut beta is also an upper bound, i.e. for every alpha in S, alpha is a subset of beta. For any rational p, if p is an element of lambda, then there is an alpha in S such that p is an element of alpha, so that p is an element of beta. Generalizing, lambda is a subset of beta. So lambda is the least upper bound on S.

End of proof.

So we have a bunch of entities which satisfy the Completeness Property. And one way to put this is: there are no “gaps” in our cuts. (So: taking further “cuts” of reals, rather than rationals, would yield no interesting new objects.)

Next, we must define some operations on the reals. We start by embedding the rationals into the reals by stipulating that the real embedding of rational p is the cut of all rational q less than p for each rational p. We then define: two operations on nonnegative cuts. Alpha plus beta is the set of p plus q for p in alpha and q in beta. Alpha times beta is the union of the real-zero cut with all products p times q for nonnegative p in alpha and nonnegative q in beta To handle the other multiplication cases, first let: negative alpha is the set of all p minus q such that p is negative and q is not in alpha and then stipulate: alpha times beta is defined in three remaining sign cases. If alpha and beta are both negative, use negative alpha times negative beta. If alpha is negative and beta is positive, take the negative of negative alpha times beta. If alpha is positive and beta is negative, take the negative of alpha times negative beta We then need to check that each of these definitions always yields a cut. And finally, we need to go through an easy (but long-winded) demonstration that the cuts, so defined, behave exactly as they should. But we relegate all of this to the Ordered Rings and Fields section.

Some Philosophical Reflections

So much for the technicalities. But what did they achieve?

Well, pretty uncontestably, they gave us some lovely pure mathematics. Moreover, there were some deep conceptual achievements. It was a profound insight, to see that the Completeness Property expresses the crucial difference between the reals and the rationals. Moreover, the explicit construction of reals, as Dedekind cuts, puts the subject matter of analysis on a firm footing. We know that the notion of a complete ordered field is coherent, for the cuts form just such a field.

For all that, we should air a few reservations about these achievements.

First, it is not clear that thinking of reals in terms of cuts is any more rigorous than thinking of reals in terms of their familiar (possibly infinite) decimal expansions. This latter “construction” of the reals has some resemblance to the construction of the reals via Cauchy sequence; but in fact, it was essentially known to mathematicians from the early 17th century onwards (see the appendix on the reals as Cauchy sequences). The real increase in rigour came from the realisation that the reals have the Completeness Property; the ability to construct real numbers as particular sets is perhaps not, by itself, so very interesting.

It is even less clear that the (much easier) arithmetization of the integers, or of the rationals, increases rigour in those areas. Here, it is worth making a simple observation. Having constructed the integers as equivalence classes of ordered pairs of naturals, and then constructed the rationals as equivalence classes of ordered pairs of integers, and then constructed the reals as sets of rationals, we immediately forget about the constructions. In particular: no one would ever want to invoke these constructions during a mathematical proof (excepting, of course, a proof that the constructions behaved as they were supposed to). It's much easier to speak about a real, directly, than to speak about some set of sets of sets of sets of sets of sets of sets of naturals.

It is most doubtful of all that these definitions tell us what the integers, rationals, or reals are, metaphysically speaking. That is, it is doubtful that the reals (say) are certain sets (of sets of sets and so on ). The main barrier to such a view is that the construction could have been done in many different ways. In the case of the reals, there are some genuinely interestingly different constructions (see the appendix on the reals as Cauchy sequences). But here is a really trivial way to obtain some different constructions: as in the Philosophical Reflections section on relations, we could have defined ordered pairs slightly differently; if we had used this alternative notion of an ordered pair, then our constructions would have worked precisely as well as they did, but we would have ended up with different objects. As such, there are many rival set-theoretic constructions of the integers, the rationals, and the reals. And now it would just be arbitrary (and embarrassing) to claim that the integers (say) are these sets, rather than those. (As in the Philosophical Reflections section on relations, this is an instance of an argument made famous by Paul Benacerraf one thousand nine hundred sixty five.)

A further point is worth raising: there is something quite odd about our constructions. We started with the natural numbers. We then construct the integers, and construct “the zero of the integers”, i.e., the integer-equivalence class of zero comma zero. But zero is not equal to the equivalence class of zero comma zero modulo integer equivalence. Indeed, given our constructions, no natural number is an integer. But that seems extremely counter-intuitive. Indeed, in the Some Important Sets section, we claimed without much argument that the set of natural numbers is a subset of the rational numbers. If the constructions tell us exactly what the numbers are, this claim was trivially false.

Standing back, then, where do we get to? Working in a naïve set theory, and helping ourselves to the naturals, we are able to treat integers, rationals, and reals as certain sets. In that sense, we can embed the theories of these entities within a set theory. But the philosophical import of this embedding is just not that straightforward.

Of course, none of this is the last word! The point is only this. Showing that the arithmetization of the reals is of deep philosophical significance would require some additional philosophical argument.

Ordered Rings and Fields

Throughout this chapter, we claimed that certain definitions behave “as they ought”. In this technical appendix, we will spell out what we mean, and (sketch how to) show that the definitions do behave “correctly”.

In the From the Natural Numbers to the Integers section, we defined addition and multiplication on the integers. We want to show that, as defined, they endow the integers with the structure we “would want” it to have. In particular, the structure in question is that of a commutative ring.

Definition of a commutative ring

A commutative ring is a set S, equipped with specific elements zero and one and operations plus and times, satisfying these eight formulas: the eight commutative-ring laws: associativity of addition and multiplication; commutativity of addition and multiplication; additive identity zero; multiplicative identity one; existence of an additive inverse for each a; and distributivity of multiplication over addition Implicitly, these are all bound with universal quantifiers restricted to S. And note that the elements zero and one here need not be the natural numbers with the same name.

So, to check that the integers form a commutative ring, we just need to check that we meet these eight conditions. None of the conditions is difficult to establish, but this is a bit laborious. For example, here is how to prove Associativity, in the case of addition:

Proof

Fix integers i, j, and k. So there are natural numbers a sub one, b sub one, a sub two, b sub two, a sub three, and b sub three such that i equals the equivalence class of a sub one comma b sub one and j equals the equivalence class of a sub two comma b sub two and k equals the equivalence class of a sub three comma b sub three. (For legibility, we write “the equivalence class of x comma y” rather than “the integer-equivalence class of x comma y”; we'll do this throughout this section.) Now: seven-line associativity calculation. First, i plus the quantity j plus k equals the integer-equivalence class of a sub one comma b sub one, plus the sum of the classes of a sub two comma b sub two and a sub three comma b sub three. Second, combine the last two classes coordinatewise. Third, reassociate both natural-number coordinates. Fourth, use associativity of natural-number addition. Fifth, split the resulting class back into two classes. Sixth, identify those classes as i, j, and k. Seventh, the result is the quantity i plus j, plus k helping ourselves freely to the behavior of addition on the natural numbers.

End of proof.

Equally, here is how to prove Additive Inverse:

Proof

Fix integer i, so that i equals the integer-equivalence class of a comma b for some natural numbers a and b. Let j be the integer-equivalence class of b comma a, an element of the integers. Helping ourselves to the behaviour of the naturals, the quantity a plus b, plus zero, equals zero plus the quantity a plus b, so that the pair a plus b comma b plus a is integer-equivalent to the pair zero comma zero by definition, and hence the equivalence class of a plus b comma b plus a equals the equivalence class of zero comma zero equals zero under the integer embedding. So now i plus j equals the equivalence class of a comma b plus the equivalence class of b comma a equals the equivalence class of a plus b comma b plus a equals the equivalence class of zero comma zero equals zero under the integer embedding.

End of proof.

And here is a proof of Distributivity:

Proof

As above, fix i equal to the integer-equivalence class of a sub one comma b sub one and j equal to the integer-equivalence class of a sub two comma b sub two and k equal to the integer-equivalence class of a sub three comma b sub three. Now: seven-line distributivity calculation. Line one: i times open parenthesis j plus k close parenthesis equals the class of a sub one comma b sub one, times the sum of the classes of a sub two comma b sub two and a sub three comma b sub three. Line two: combine that sum coordinatewise. Line three: multiply the resulting classes, obtaining first coordinate a sub one times open parenthesis a sub two plus a sub three close parenthesis plus b sub one times open parenthesis b sub two plus b sub three close parenthesis, and second coordinate a sub one times open parenthesis b sub two plus b sub three close parenthesis plus b sub one times open parenthesis a sub two plus a sub three close parenthesis. Line four: after distribution, the first coordinate is a sub one times a sub two plus a sub one times a sub three plus b sub one times b sub two plus b sub one times b sub three, and the second coordinate is a sub one times b sub two plus a sub one times b sub three plus a sub two times b sub one plus a sub three times b sub one. Line five: split the class into the class with coordinates a sub one times a sub two plus b sub one times b sub two, and a sub one times b sub two plus a sub two times b sub one, plus the analogous class using subscript three. Line six: these are the product of i with j plus the product of i with k. Line seven: the result is i times j plus i times k

End of proof.

We leave it as an exercise to prove the remaining five conditions. Having done that, we have shown that the integer structure constitutes a commutative ring, i.e., that addition and multiplication (as defined) behave as they should.

But our task is not over. As well as defining addition and multiplication over the integers, we defined an ordering relation, the less-than-or-equal relation, and we must check that this behaves as it should. In more detail, we must show that the integer structure constitutes an ordered ring.Recall from the definition of a linear order that a total order is a relation which is reflexive, transitive, anti-symmetric, and connected. In the context of order relations, connectedness is sometimes called trichotomy, since for any a and b we have either a is at most b, a equals b, or a is at least b.

Definition of an ordered ring

An ordered ring is a commutative ring which is also equipped with a total order relation, the less-than-or-equal relation, such that: two ordered-ring compatibility laws: if a is at most b, then a plus c is at most b plus c; and if a is at most b while c is nonnegative, then a times c is at most b times c

This takes care of the integers. But now we need to show very similar things of the rationals. In particular, we now need to show that the rationals form an ordered field, under our given definitions of plus, times, and the less-than-or-equal relation:

Definition of an ordered field

An ordered field is an ordered ring which also satisfies: for every nonzero a in S there exists b in S such that a times b equals one

Once you have shown that the integer structure constitutes an ordered ring, it is easy but laborious to show that the rational-number structure constitutes an ordered field.

Having dealt with the integers and the rationals, it only remains to deal with the reals. In particular, we need to show that the real-number structure constitutes a complete ordered field, i.e., an ordered field with the Completeness Property. Now, the theorem that the set of cuts has the Completeness Property established that the real-number structure has the Completeness Property. However, it remains to run through the (tedious) details of checking that the real-number structure is an ordered field.

Before tearing off into that laborious exercise, we need to check some more “immediate” things. For example, we need a guarantee that alpha plus beta, as defined, is indeed a cut, for any cuts alpha and beta. Here is a proof of that fact:

Proof

Since alpha and beta are both cuts, alpha plus beta, namely the set of p plus q such that p is in alpha and q is in beta is a non-empty proper subset of the rational numbers. Now suppose x is less than p plus q for some p in alpha and q in beta. Then x minus p is less than q, so x minus p is an element of beta, and x equals p plus the quantity x minus p, and x belongs to alpha plus beta. So alpha plus beta is an initial segment of the rational numbers. Finally, for any member p plus q of alpha plus beta, since alpha and beta are both cuts, there are elements p sub one of alpha and q sub one of beta such that p is less than p sub one and q is less than q sub one; so p plus q is less than p sub one plus q sub one, which belongs to alpha plus beta; so alpha plus beta has no maximum.

End of proof.

Similar efforts will allow you to check that alpha minus beta and alpha times beta and alpha divided by beta are cuts (in the last case, ignoring the case where beta is the zero-cut). Again, though, we will simply leave this to you.

Appendix: the Reals as Cauchy Sequences

In the From the Rationals to the Reals section, we constructed the reals as Dedekind cuts. In this section, we explain an alternative construction. It builds on Cauchy's definition of (what we now call) a Cauchy sequence; but the use of this definition to construct the reals is due to other nineteenth-century authors, notably Weierstrass, Heine, Méray and Cantor. (For a nice history, see John J. O'Connor and Edmund F. Robertson two thousand five.)

Before we get to the nineteenth century, it's worth considering Simon Stevin (1548–1620). In brief, Stevin realised that we can think of each real in terms of its decimal expansion. Thus even an irrational number, like the square root of two, has a nice decimal expansion, beginning: one point four one four two one three five six two three seven and so on It is very easy to model decimal expansions in set theory: simply consider them as functions d from the natural numbers to the natural numbers, where d of n is the n-th decimal place that we are interested in. We will then need a bit of tweak, to handle the bit of the real number that comes before the decimal point (here, just one). We will also need a further tweak (an equivalence relation) to guarantee that, for example, zero point nine nine nine and so on equals one. But it is not difficult to offer a perfectly rigorous construction of the real numbers, in the manner of Stevin, within set theory.

Stevin is not our focus. (For more on Stevin, see Karin Usadi Katz and Mikhail G. Katz two thousand twelve.) But here is a closely related thought. Instead of treating the square root of two's decimal expansion directly, we can instead consider a sequence of increasingly accurate rational approximations to the square root of two, by considering the increasingly precise expansions: one, one point four, one point four one four, one point four one four two, one point four one four two one, and so on The idea that reals can be considered via “increasingly good approximations” provides us with the basis for another sequence of insights (akin to the realisations that we used when constructing the rational numbers from the integers, or the integers from the natural numbers). The basic insights are these:

  1. Every real can be written as a (perhaps infinite) decimal expansion.

  2. The information encoded by a (perhaps infinite) decimal expansion can be equally be encoded by a sequence of rational numbers.

  3. A sequence of rational numbers can be thought of as a function from the natural numbers to the rational numbers; just let f of n be the n-th rational in the sequence.

Of course, not just any function from the natural numbers to the rational numbers will give us a real number. For instance, consider this function: f of n equals one when n is odd, and zero when n is even Essentially the worry here is that the sequence zero, one, zero, one, zero, one, zero, and so on doesn't seem to “hone in” on any real. So: to ensure that we consider sequences which do hone in on some real, we need to restrict our attention to sequences which have some limit.

We have already encountered the idea of a limit, in the Rigorous Definition of Limits section. But we cannot use quite the same definition as we used there. The expression “for every epsilon greater than zero” there tacitly involved quantification over the real numbers; and we were considering the limits of functions on the real numbers; so invoking that definition would be to help ourselves to the real numbers; and they are exactly what we were aiming to construct. Fortunately, we can work with a closely related idea of a limit.

Definition of a Cauchy sequence

A function f from the natural numbers to the rational numbers is a Cauchy sequence iff for any positive rational epsilon we have that there exists a natural-number index ell such that for every m and n greater than ell, the absolute value of f of m minus f of n is less than epsilon.

The general idea of a limit is the same as before: if you want a certain level of precision (measured by epsilon), there is a “region” to look in (any input greater than ell). And it is easy to see that our sequence one, one point four, one point four one four, one point four one four two, one point four one four two one and so on has a limit: if you want to approximate the square root of two to within an error of the fraction with numerator one and denominator ten to the power n, then just look to any entry after the n-th.

The obvious thought, then, would be to say that a real number just is any Cauchy sequence. But, as in the constructions of the integers and the rational numbers, this would be too naïve: for any given real number, multiple different Cauchy sequences indicate that real number. A simple way to see this is as follows. Given a Cauchy sequence f, define g to be exactly the same function as f, except that g of zero is not equal to f of zero. Since the two sequences agree everywhere after the first number, we will (ultimately) want to say that they have the same limit, in the sense employed in the definition of a Cauchy sequence, and so should be thought of “defining” the same real. So, we should really think of these Cauchy sequences as the same real number.

Consequently, we again need to define an equivalence relation on the Cauchy sequences, and identify real numbers with equivalence classes. First we need the idea of a function which tends to zero in the limit. For any function h from the natural numbers to the rational numbers, say that h tends to zero iff for any positive rational epsilon we have that there exists a natural-number index ell such that for every n greater than ell, the absolute value of h of n is less than epsilon.Compare this with the definition of the limit of f of x as x approaches infinity equals zero in the Rigorous Definition of Limits section. Further, where f and g are functions from the natural numbers to the rational numbers, let f minus g, evaluated at n, equals f of n minus g of n. Now define: f is real-equivalent to g if and only if f minus g tends to zero We need to check that real equivalence is an equivalence relation; and it is. We can then, if we like, define the reals as the equivalence classes, under real equivalence, of all Cauchy sequences from the natural numbers to the rational numbers.

Having done this, we shall as usual write the real-equivalence class of f for the equivalence class with f as an element. However, to keep things readable, in what follows we will drop the subscript and write just the real-equivalence class of f. We also stipulate that, for each rational q, we have q under the real embedding equals the equivalence class of c sub q, where c sub q is the constant function c sub q of n equals q for all n in the natural numbers. We then define basic relations and operations on the reals, e.g.: the sum of the real-equivalence classes of f and g is the class of f plus g, and their product is the class of f times g where f plus g, evaluated at n, equals f of n plus g of n and f times g, evaluated at n, equals f of n times g of n. Of course, we also need to check that each of the pointwise sum f plus g, the pointwise difference f minus g and the pointwise product f times g are Cauchy sequences when f and g are; but they are, and we leave this to you.

Finally, we define a notion of order. Say the real-equivalence class of f is positive iff both the real-equivalence class of f is not equal to real zero and there exists a natural-number index ell such that f of n is positive for every n greater than ell. Then say the real-equivalence class of f is less than the real-equivalence class of g iff the real-equivalence class of g minus f is positive. We have to check that this is well-defined (i.e., that it does not depend upon choice of “representative” function from the equivalence class).

Prove that the Cauchy sequences constitute an ordered field.

It is harder to prove that the reals, so constructed, have the Completeness Property, so we will give the proof.

Completeness of the Cauchy-sequence reals

Every non-empty set of Cauchy sequences with an upper bound has a least upper bound.

Proof sketch

Let S be any non-empty set of Cauchy sequences with an upper bound. So there is some rational p such that p under the real embedding is an upper bound for S. Let r be an element of S; then there is some rational q such that the real embedding of q is less than the real represented by r. So if a least upper bound on S exists, it is between q under the real embedding and p under the real embedding (inclusive).

We will hone in on the l.u.b., by approaching it simultaneously from below and above. In particular, we define two functions, f and g from the natural numbers to the rational numbers, with the aim that f will hone in on the l.u.b. from above, and g will home in on it from below. We start by defining: f of zero equals p; g of zero equals q Then, where a sub n equals the average of f of n and g of n, let:This is a recursive definition. But we have not yet given any reason to think that recursive definitions are ok. two recursive clauses. f of n plus one equals a sub n if every h in S has class at most the real embedding of a sub n, and otherwise equals f of n. g of n plus one equals a sub n if some h in S has class at least the real embedding of a sub n, and otherwise equals g of n Both f and g are Cauchy sequences. (This can be checked fairly easily, but we leave it as an exercise.) Note that the function f minus g tends to zero, since the difference between f and g halves at each step. Hence the real-equivalence class of f equals the real-equivalence class of g.

We first show that the real-equivalence class of f is an upper bound on S, i.e. that for every h in S, the real-equivalence class of h is at most the real-equivalence class of f. (We will invoke the theorem that the Cauchy-sequence reals form an ordered field as we go.) Let h be an element of S and suppose, for reductio, that the real-equivalence class of f is less than the real-equivalence class of h, so that real zero is less than the real-equivalence class of h minus f. Since f is a monotonically decreasing Cauchy sequence, there is some natural number n such that the real-equivalence class of c sub f of n minus f is less than the real-equivalence class of h minus f. So: the real embedding of f of n equals the class of the constant sequence at f of n, which is less than the class of f plus the class of h minus f, which equals the class of h contradicting the fact that, by construction, the real-equivalence class of h is at most the real embedding of f of n.

We next show that the common real-equivalence class represented by f and g; equivalently, the common real-equivalence class represented by f and g is the least upper bound on S. So let j be any Cauchy sequence and suppose the real-equivalence class of j is less than the real-equivalence class of g. Reasoning as above (using the fact that g is increasing), there is a natural number n such that the real-equivalence class of j is less than the real embedding of g of n. But by construction there is an h in S such that the real embedding of g of n is at most the real-equivalence class of h, so the real-equivalence class of j is less than the real-equivalence class of h and therefore the real-equivalence class of j is not an upper bound on S.

End of proof.
