Chapter. Cardinals.

Section. Cantor's Principle. Cast your mind back to section Von Neumann's Construction of the Ordinals in chapter Ordinals . We were discussing well-ordered sets, and suggested that it would be nice to have objects which go proxy for well-orders. With this is mind, we introduced ordinals, and then showed in corollary six in chapter Ordinals that these behave as we would want them to, that is: Displayed expression. the order type of capital A is less than equals the order type of capital B is strictly smaller than if and only if the ordered pair capital A, then is less than is isomorphic to the ordered pair capital B, then is strictly smaller than. End displayed expression. Cast your mind back even further, to section Equinumerosity in chapter The Size of Sets . There, working naïvely, we introduced the notion of the “size” of a set. Specifically, we said that two sets are equinumerous, capital A is equinumerous with capital B , just in case there is a bijection f colon capital A maps to capital B . This is an intrinsically simpler notion than that of a well-ordering: we are only interested in bijections, and not (as with order-isomorphisms) whether the bijections “preserve any structure”. This all gives rise to an obvious thought. Just as we introduced certain objects, ordinals, to calibrate well-orders, we can introduce certain objects, cardinals, to calibrate size. That is the aim of this chapter. Before we say what these cardinals will be, we should lay down a principle which they ought to satisfy. Writing the cardinality of capital X for the cardinality of the set capital X , we would want them to obey: Displayed expression. the cardinality of capital A equals the cardinality of capital B if and only if capital A is equinumerous with capital B. End displayed expression. We'll call this Cantor's Principle, since Cantor was probably the first to have it very clearly in mind. (We'll say more about its relationship to Hume's Principle in section Appendix: Hume's Principle in chapter Cardinals .) So our aim is to define the cardinality of capital X , for each capital X , in such a way that it delivers Cantor's Principle. End section.

Section. Cardinals as Ordinals. In fact, our theory of cardinals will just make (shameless) use of our theory of ordinals. That is: we will just define cardinals as certain specific ordinals. In particular, we will offer the following: Definition. If capital A can be well-ordered, then the cardinality of capital A is the least ordinal gamma such that capital A is equinumerous with gamma . For any ordinal gamma , we say that gamma is a cardinal if and only if gamma equals the cardinality of gamma . End definition. We just used the phrase “ capital A can be well-ordered”. As is almost always the case in mathematics, the modal locution here is just a hand-waving gloss on an existential claim: to say “ capital A can be well-ordered” is just to say “there is a relation which well-orders capital A ”. But there is a snag with definition one in chapter Cardinals . We would like it to be the case that every set has a size, that is, that the cardinality of capital A exists for every capital A . The definition we just gave, though, begins with a conditional: “ If capital A can be well-ordered and so on ”. If there is some set capital A which cannot be well-ordered, then our definition will simply fail to define an object the cardinality of capital A . So, to use definition one in chapter Cardinals , we need a guarantee that every set can be well-ordered. Sadly, though, this guarantee is unavailable in set theory Z F . So, if we want to use definition one in chapter Cardinals , there is no alternative but to add a new axiom, such as: Axiom. Well-Ordering. Every set can be well-ordered. End axiom. We will discuss whether the Well-Ordering Axiom is acceptable in chapter Choice . From now on, though, we will simply help ourselves to it. And, using it, it is quite straightforward to prove that cardinals (as defined in definition one in chapter Cardinals ) exist and behave nicely: Lemma. For every set capital A : List. Next item. the cardinality of capital A exists and is unique; Next item. the cardinality of capital A is equinumerous with capital A ; Next item. the cardinality of capital A is a cardinal, that is, the cardinality of capital A equals the cardinality of the cardinality of capital A ; End list. End lemma. Proof. Fix capital A . By Well-Ordering, there is a well-ordering the ordered pair capital A, then capital R . By theorem five in chapter Ordinals , the ordered pair capital A, then capital R is isomorphic to a unique ordinal, beta . So capital A is equinumerous with beta . By Transfinite Induction, there is a uniquely least ordinal, gamma , such that capital A is equinumerous with gamma . So the cardinality of capital A equals gamma , establishing item 1 of lemma one in chapter Cardinals and item 2 of lemma one in chapter Cardinals . To establish item 3 of lemma one in chapter Cardinals , note that if delta belongs to gamma then delta has smaller cardinality than capital A , by our choice of gamma , so that also delta has smaller cardinality than gamma since equinumerosity is an equivalence relation ( proposition Equinumerosity as an equivalence relation in chapter The Size of Sets ). So gamma equals the cardinality of gamma . End proof. The next result guarantees Cantor's Principle, and more besides. (Note that cardinals inherit their ordering from the ordinals, that is, the cardinal a is less than the cardinal b if and only if the cardinal a belongs to the cardinal b . In formulating this, we will use Fraktur letters for objects we know to be cardinals. This is fairly standard. A common alternative is to use Greek letters, since cardinals are ordinals, but to choose them from the middle of the alphabet, for example: kappa, then lambda .): Lemma. For any sets capital A and capital B : Displayed expression. Source-ordered display. capital A is equinumerous with capital B if and only if the cardinality of capital A equals the cardinality of capital B. Then, capital A has cardinality at most that of capital B if and only if the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to the cardinality of capital B. Then, capital A has smaller cardinality than capital B if and only if the cardinality of capital A is less than the cardinality of capital B. End display. End displayed expression. End lemma. Proof. We will prove the left-to-right direction of the second claim (the other cases are similar, and left as an exercise). So, consider the following diagram: Commutative comparison diagram. On the top row, capital A maps by an injection to capital B . Vertical bijections connect those two sets, respectively, with the cardinality of capital A and the cardinality of capital B . A dashed bottom arrow is the resulting injection between the two cardinalities. End of diagram. The double-headed arrows indicate bijections, whose existence is guaranteed by the lemma on Cardinals Exist . In assuming that capital A has cardinality at most that of capital B , there is an injection capital A maps to capital B . Now, chasing the arrows around from the cardinality of capital A to capital A to capital B to the cardinality of capital B , we obtain an injection the cardinality of capital A maps to the cardinality of capital B (the dashed arrow). End proof. We can also use the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right to re-prove Schröder—Bernstein. This is the claim that if capital A has cardinality at most that of capital B and capital B has cardinality at most that of capital A then capital A is equinumerous with capital B . We stated this as the theorem on schroder bernstein , but first proved it—with some effort—in section Appendix: Proving Schröder-Bernstein in chapter Infinite Sets . Now consider: Proof. Re-proof of Schröder-Bernstein. If capital A has cardinality at most that of capital B and capital B has cardinality at most that of capital A , then the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to the cardinality of capital B and the cardinality of capital B is less than or equal to the cardinality of capital A by the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right . So the cardinality of capital A equals the cardinality of capital B and capital A is equinumerous with capital B by Trichotomy and the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right . End proof. Whilst this is a very simple proof, it implicitly relies on both Replacement (to secure theorem five in chapter Ordinals ) and on Well-Ordering (to guarantee the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right ). By contrast, the proof of section Appendix: Proving Schröder-Bernstein in chapter Infinite Sets was much more self-standing (indeed, it can be carried out in set theory Z minus ). End section.

Section. set theory Z F C : A Milestone. With the addition of Well-Ordering, we have reached the final theoretical milestone. We now have all the axioms required for set theory Z F C . In detail: Definition. The theory set theory Z F C has these axioms: Extensionality, Union, Pairs, Powersets, Infinity, Foundation, Well-Ordering and all instances of the Separation and Replacement schemes. Otherwise put, set theory Z F C adds Well-Ordering to set theory Z F . End definition. set theory Z F C stands for Zermelo—Fraenkel set theory with Choice. Now this might seem slightly odd, since the axiom we added was called “Well-Ordering”, not “Choice”. But, when we later formulate Choice, it will turn out that Well-Ordering is equivalent (modulo set theory Z F ) to Choice (see theorem in set theory Z F in chapter Choice ). So which to take as our “basic” axiom is a matter of indifference. And the name “ set theory Z F C ” is entirely standard in the literature. End section.

Section. Finite, Enumerable, Non-enumerable. Now that we have been introduced to cardinals, it is worth spending a little time talking about different varieties of cardinals; specifically, finite, enumerable, and non-enumerable cardinals. Our first two results entail that the finite cardinals will be exactly the finite ordinals, which we defined as our natural numbers back in definition of the natural numbers and omega in chapter Steps towards set theory Z : Proposition. Let n, then m belongs to omega . Then n equals m if and only if n is equinumerous with m . End proposition. Proof. Left-to-right is trivial. To prove right-to-left, suppose n is equinumerous with m although n is not equal to m . By Trichotomy, either n belongs to m or m belongs to n ; suppose n belongs to m without loss of generality. Then n is a proper subset of m and there is a bijection f colon m maps to n , so that m is Dedekind infinite, contradicting proposition that natural numbers are not Dedekind infinite in chapter Steps towards set theory Z . End proof. Corollary. If n belongs to omega , then n is a cardinal. End corollary. Proof. Immediate. End proof. It also follows that several reasonable notions of what it might mean to describe a cardinal as “finite” or “infinite” coincide: Theorem. For any set capital A , the following are equivalent: List. Next item. the cardinality of capital A does not belong to omega , that is, capital A is not a natural number; Next item. omega is less than or equal to the cardinality of capital A ; Next item. capital A is Dedekind infinite. End list. End theorem. Proof. From lemma five in chapter Ordinal Arithmetic , the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right , and corollary one in chapter Cardinals . End proof. This licenses the following definition of some notions which we used rather informally in part Naïve Set Theory : Definition. We say that capital A is finite if and only if the cardinality of capital A is a natural number, that is, the cardinality of capital A belongs to omega . Otherwise, we say that capital A is infinite. End definition. But note that this definition is presented against the background of set theory Z F C . After all, we needed Well-Ordering to guarantee that every set has a cardinality. And indeed, without Well-Ordering, there can be a set which is neither finite nor Dedekind infinite. We will return to this sort of issue in chapter Choice . For now, we continue to rely upon Well-Ordering. Let us now turn from the finite cardinals to the infinite cardinals. Here are two elementary points: Corollary. omega is the least infinite cardinal. End corollary. Proof. omega is a cardinal, since omega is Dedekind infinite and if omega is equinumerous with n for any n belongs to omega then n would be Dedekind infinite, contradicting proposition that natural numbers are not Dedekind infinite in chapter Steps towards set theory Z . Now omega is the least infinite cardinal by definition. End proof. Corollary. Every infinite cardinal is a limit ordinal. End corollary. Proof. Let alpha be an infinite successor ordinal, so alpha equals beta ordinal plus one for some beta . By proposition one in chapter Cardinals , beta is also infinite, so beta is equinumerous with beta ordinal plus one by lemma five in chapter Ordinal Arithmetic . Now the cardinality of beta equals the cardinality of beta ordinal plus one by the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right , so that alpha is not equal to the cardinality of alpha . End proof. Now, as early as the definition on enumerable , we flagged we can distinguish between enumerable and non-enumerable infinite sets. That definition naturally leads to the following: Proposition. capital A is enumerable if and only if the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to omega , and capital A is non-enumerable if and only if omega is less than the cardinality of capital A . End proposition. Proof. By Trichotomy, the two claims are equivalent, so it suffices to prove that capital A is enumerable if and only if the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to omega . For right-to-left: if the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to omega , then capital A has cardinality at most that of omega by the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right and corollary two in chapter Cardinals . For left-to-right: suppose capital A is enumerable; then by the definition on enumerable there are three possible cases: List. Next item. if capital A equals the empty set , then the cardinality of capital A equals zero belongs to omega , by corollary one in chapter Cardinals and the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right . Next item. if n is equinumerous with capital A , then the cardinality of capital A equals n belongs to omega , by corollary one in chapter Cardinals and the lemma on Cardinals Behave Right . Next item. if omega is equinumerous with capital A , then the cardinality of capital A equals omega , by corollary two in chapter Cardinals . End list. So in all cases, the cardinality of capital A is less than or equal to omega . End proof. Indeed, omega has a special place. Whilst there are many countable ordinals: Corollary. omega is the only enumerable infinite cardinal. End corollary. Proof. Let the cardinal a be an enumerable infinite cardinal. Since the cardinal a is infinite, omega is less than or equal to the cardinal a . Since the cardinal a is an enumerable cardinal, the cardinal a equals the cardinality of the cardinal a is less than or equal to omega . So the cardinal a equals omega by Trichotomy. End proof. Of course, there are infinitely many cardinals. So we might ask: How many cardinals are there? The following results show that we might want to reconsider that question. Proposition. If every member of capital X is a cardinal, then the union of capital X is a cardinal. End proposition. Proof. It is easy to check that the union of capital X is an ordinal. Let alpha belongs to the union of capital X be an ordinal; then alpha belongs to the cardinal b belongs to capital X for some cardinal the cardinal b . Since the cardinal b is a cardinal, alpha has smaller cardinality than the cardinal b . Since the cardinal b is a subset of the union of capital X , we have the cardinal b has cardinality at most that of the union of capital X , and so alpha is not equinumerous with the union of capital X . Generalising, the union of capital X is a cardinal. End proof. Theorem. There is no largest cardinal. End theorem. Proof. For any cardinal the cardinal a , Cantor's Theorem ( the theorem on cantor ) and the lemma on Cardinals Exist entail that the cardinal a is less than the cardinality of the power set of the cardinal a . End proof. Theorem. The set of all cardinals does not exist. End theorem. Proof. For reductio, suppose capital C equals the set of the cardinal a such that the cardinal a is a cardinal . Now the union of capital C is a cardinal by proposition three in chapter Cardinals , so by the lemma on No Largest Cardinal there is a cardinal the cardinal b is greater than the union of capital C . By definition the cardinal b belongs to capital C , so the cardinal b is a subset of the union of capital C , so that the cardinal b is less than or equal to the union of capital C , a contradiction. End proof. You should compare this with both Russell's Paradox and Burali-Forti. End section.

Section. Appendix: Hume's Principle. In section Cantor's Principle in chapter Cardinals , we described Cantor's Principle. This was: Displayed expression. Source-ordered display. the cardinality of capital A equals the cardinality of capital B if and only if capital A is approximately equal to capital B. Then, This is very similar to what is now called Hume's Principle , which says:. Then, the Frege number of x capital F of x equals the Frege number of x capital G of x if and only if capital F is equivalent to capital G. End display. End displayed expression. where ` capital F is equivalent to capital G ' abbreviates that there are exactly as many capital F s as capital G s, that is, the capital F s can be put into a bijection with the capital G s, that is: Displayed expression. there exists a relation capital R such that three conditions hold. First, whenever capital R relates v to y, capital F holds of v and capital G holds of y. Second, every v satisfying capital F is related by capital R to exactly one y. Third, every y satisfying capital G is related by capital R to exactly one v. End conditions. End displayed expression. But there is a type-difference between Hume's Principle and Cantor's Principle. In the statement of Cantor's Principle, the variables “ capital A ” and “ capital B ” are first-order terms which stand for sets. In the statement of Hume's Principle, “ capital F ”, “ capital G ” and “ capital R ” are not first-order terms; rather, they are in predicate position. (Maybe they stand for properties.) So we might gloss Hume's Principle in English as: the number of capital F s is the number of capital G s if and only if the capital F s are bijective with the capital G s. This is called Hume's Principle, because Hume once wrote this: Quotation. When two numbers are so combined as that the one has always an unit answering to every unit of the other, we pronounce them equal. (David Hume, one thousand seven hundred forty, part three book one section one) End quotation. And Hume's Principle was brought to contemporary mathematico-logical prominence by Gottlob Frege (one thousand eight hundred eighty four), section sixty three , who quoted this passage from Hume, before (in effect) sketching (what we have called) Hume's Principle. You should note the structural similarity between Hume's Principle and Basic Law V. We formulated this in section Appendix: Frege's Basic Law V in chapter The Iterative Conception as follows: Displayed expression. the Frege extension of x capital F of x equals the Frege extension of x capital G of x, iff, for every x, open scope, capital F of x if and only if capital G of x, close scope. End displayed expression. And, at this point, some commentary and comparison might help. There are two ways to take a principle like Hume's Principle or Basic Law V: predicatively or impredicatively (recall section Predicative and Impredicative in chapter The Iterative Conception ). On the impredicative reading of Basic Law V, for each capital F , the object the Frege extension of x capital F of x falls within the domain of quantification that we used in formulating Basic Law V itself. Similarly, on the impredicative reading of Hume's Principle, for each capital F , the object the Frege number of x capital F of x falls within the domain of quantification that we used in formulating Hume's Principle. By contrast, on the predicative understanding, the objects the Frege extension of x capital F of x and the Frege number of x capital F of x would be entities from some different domain. Now, if we read Basic Law V impredicatively, it leads to inconsistency, via Naïve Comprehension (for the details, see section Appendix: Frege's Basic Law V in chapter The Iterative Conception ). Much like Naïve Comprehension, it can be rendered consistent by reading it predicatively. But it probably will not do everything that we wanted it to. Hume's Principle, however, can consistently be read impredicatively. And, read thus, it is quite powerful. To illustrate: consider the predicate “ x is not equal to x ”, which obviously nothing satisfies. Hume's Principle now yields an object the number of x such that x is not equal to x . We might treat this as the number zero . Now, on the impredicative understanding—but only on the impredicative understanding—this entity zero falls within our original domain of quantification. So we can sensibly apply Hume's Principle with the predicate “ x equals zero ” to obtain an object the number of x such that x equals zero . We might treat this as the number one . Moreover, Hume's Principle entails that zero is not equal to one , since there cannot be a bijection from the non-self-identical objects to the objects identical with zero (there are none of the former, but one of the latter). Now, working impredicatively again, one falls within our original domain of quantification. So we can sensibly apply Hume's Principle with the predicate “ open scope, x equals zero or x equals one, close scope ” to obtain an object the number of x such that x equals zero or x equals one . We might treat this as the number two , and we can show that zero is not equal to two and one is not equal to two and so on. In short, taken impredicatively, Hume's Principle entails that there are infinitely many objects. And this has encouraged neo-Fregean logicists to take Hume's Principle as the foundation for arithmetic. Frege himself, though, did not take Hume's Principle as his foundation for arithmetic. Instead, Frege proved Hume's Principle from an explicit definition: the Frege number of x capital F of x is defined as the extension of the concept capital F is equivalent to capital Phi . In modern terms, we might attempt to render this as the Frege number of x capital F of x equals the set of capital G such that capital F is equivalent to capital G ; but this will pull us back into the problems of Naïve Comprehension. End section. End chapter.
