Chapter. Ordinals.

Section. Introduction. In chapter Steps towards set theory Z , we postulated that there is an infinite-th stage of the hierarchy, in the form of Stages hit infinity (see also our axiom of Infinity). However, given Stages keep going, we can't stop at the infinite-th stage; we have to keep going. So: at the next stage after the first infinite stage, we form all possible collections of sets that were available at the first infinite stage; and repeat; and repeat; and repeat; and so on Implicitly what has happened here is that we have started to invoke an “intuitive” notion of number, according to which there can be numbers after all the natural numbers. In particular, the notion involved is that of a transfinite ordinal. The aim of this chapter is to make this idea more rigorous. We will explore the general notion of an ordinal, and then explicitly define certain sets to be our ordinals. End section.

These canonical sets were first introduced by von Neumann: Definition. The set capital A is transitive if and only if for every x in capital A, x is a subset of capital A . Section. Basic Properties of the Ordinals. We observed that the first few ordinals are the natural numbers. Section. Replacement. In section Von Neumann's Construction of the Ordinals in chapter Ordinals , we motivated the introduction of ordinals by suggesting that we could treat them as order-types, that is, canonical proxies for well-orderings. In order for that to work, we would need to prove that every well-ordering is isomorphic to some ordinal. This would allow us to define the order type of capital A under the less-than relation as the ordinal alpha such that capital A under less-than is isomorphic to alpha . Unfortunately, we cannot prove the desired result only the Axioms we provided introduced so far. (We will see why in section The Strength of Replacement in chapter Replacement , but for now the point is: we can't.) We need a new thought, and here it is: Axiom. Scheme of Replacement. For any formula phi of x and y , the following is an axiom: Quotation. for any capital A , if for every x in capital A, there exists exactly one y, phi of x and y , then the set of y such that there exists x in capital A, phi of x and y exists. End quotation. End axiom. As with Separation, this is a scheme: it yields infinitely many axioms, for each of the infinitely many different phi apostrophe s . And it can equally well be (and normally is) written down thus: For any formula phi of x and y which does not contain “ capital B ”, the following is an axiom: Displayed expression. for every capital A, if every x in capital A has exactly one y such that phi holds of x and y, then there exists a capital B such that, for every y, y belongs to capital B if and only if phi holds of x and y for some x in capital A. End displayed expression. On first encounter, however, this is quite a tangled formula. The following quick consequence of Replacement probably gives a clearer expression to the intuitive idea we are working with: Footnote. A term is an expression which picks out exactly one object (on any completion of its free variables). For example, “ the empty set ” is a term which picks out the empty set; “ the set containing x ” is a term which picks out x apostrophe s singleton (whatever x might be); “ x union y ” is a term which picks out the union of x and y (whatever they might be). End footnote. Corollary. For any term tau of x , and any set capital A , this set exists: Displayed expression. the set of tau of x such that x belongs to capital A, equals the set of y such that there exists x in capital A, y equals tau of x. End displayed expression. End corollary. Proof. Since tau is a term, for every x, there exists exactly one y, tau of x equals y . A fortiori, for every x in capital A, there exists exactly one y, tau of x equals y . So the set of y such that there exists x in capital A, tau of x equals y exists by Replacement. End proof. This suggests that “Replacement” is a good name for the Axiom: given a set capital A , you can form a new set, the set of tau of x such that x belongs to capital A , by replacing every member of capital A with its image under tau . Indeed, following the notation for the image of a set under a function, we might write the image of capital A under tau for the set of tau of x such that x belongs to capital A . Crucially, however, tau is a term. It need not be (a name for) a function, in the sense of section Functions as Relations in chapter Functions , that is, a certain set of ordered pairs. After all, if f is a function (in that sense), then the set the image of capital A under f equals the set of f of x such that x belongs to capital A is just a particular subset of the range of f , and that is already guaranteed to exist, just using the axioms of set theory Z minus . Footnote. Just consider the set of y in the double union of f such that y equals f of x for some x in capital A . End footnote. Replacement, by contrast, is a powerful addition to our axioms, as we will see in chapter Replacement . End section.

If gamma belongs to beta , then gamma belongs to the domain of f by lemma three in chapter Ordinals ; generalising, beta is a subset of the domain of f . End proof. If the ordered pair consisting of capital A and the less-than relation is a well-ordering, then its order type, the order type of capital A under the less-than relation , is the unique ordinal alpha such that capital A under less-than is order-isomorphic to alpha . End definition. Moreover, this definition licenses two nice principles: Corollary. Where the ordered pair consisting of capital A and the less-than relation and the ordered pair consisting of capital B and the less-dot relation are well-orderings: Displayed expression. Two order-type principles. First, the order types are equal if and only if capital A under less-than is order-isomorphic to capital B under less-dot. Second, the first order type belongs to the second if and only if capital A under less-than is order-isomorphic to the initial segment of capital B below some b. End principles. End displayed expression. End corollary. Proof. The identity holds by proposition six in chapter Ordinals . To prove the second claim, let the order type of capital A under less-than equals alpha and the order type of capital B under less-dot equals beta , and let f maps beta isomorphically to capital B under the less-dot relation be our isomorphism. Then: Displayed expression. Three equivalent conditions. Alpha belongs to beta if and only if the restriction of f to alpha is an isomorphism from alpha to the initial segment of capital B below f of alpha. This holds if and only if capital A under less-than is order-isomorphic to that initial segment, and if and only if it is order-isomorphic to the initial segment of capital B below some b. End equivalences. End displayed expression. by proposition three in chapter Ordinals , lemma three in chapter Ordinals , and corollary two in chapter Ordinals . End proof. End section.

Section. Successor and Limit Ordinals. In the next few chapters, we will use ordinals a great deal. So it will help if we introduce some simple notions. Definition. For any ordinal alpha , its successor is the ordinal successor of alpha equals alpha union the set containing alpha . We say that alpha is a successor ordinal if the ordinal successor of beta equals alpha for some ordinal beta . We say that alpha is a limit ordinal if and only if alpha is neither empty nor a successor ordinal. End definition. The following result shows that this is the right notion of successor: Proposition. For any ordinal alpha : List. Next item. alpha belongs to the ordinal successor of alpha ; Next item. the ordinal successor of alpha is an ordinal; Next item. there is no ordinal beta such that alpha belongs to beta, and beta belongs to the ordinal successor of alpha . End list. End proposition. Proof. Trivially, alpha belongs to alpha union the set containing alpha equals the ordinal successor of alpha . Equally, the ordinal successor of alpha is a transitive set of ordinals, and hence an ordinal by corollary four in chapter Ordinals . And it is impossible that alpha belongs to beta, and beta belongs to the ordinal successor of alpha , since then either beta belongs to alpha or beta equals alpha , contradicting corollary three in chapter Ordinals . End proof. This also licenses a variant of proof by transfinite induction: Theorem. Simple Transfinite Induction. Let phi of x be a formula such that: List. Next item. phi of the empty set ; and Next item. for any ordinal alpha , if phi of alpha then phi of the ordinal successor of alpha ; and Next item. if alpha is a limit ordinal and for every beta in alpha, phi of beta , then phi of alpha . End list. Then for every alpha, phi of alpha . End theorem. Proof. We prove the contrapositive. So, suppose there is some ordinal which is not phi ; let gamma be the least such ordinal. Then either gamma equals the empty set , or gamma equals the ordinal successor of alpha for some alpha such that phi of alpha ; or gamma is a limit ordinal and for every beta in gamma, phi of beta . End proof. A final bit of notation will prove helpful later on: Definition. If capital X is a set of ordinals, then the strict supremum of capital X equals the union, over alpha in capital X, of the ordinal successors of alpha . End definition. Here, “lsub” stands for “least strict upper bound”. Footnote. Some books use “ the supremum of capital X ” for this. But other books use “ the supremum of capital X ” for the least non-strict upper bound, that is, simply the union of capital X . If capital X has a greatest element, alpha , these notions come apart: the least strict upper bound is the ordinal successor of alpha , whereas the least non-strict upper bound is just alpha . End footnote. The following result explains this: Proposition. If capital X is a set of ordinals, the strict supremum of capital X is the least ordinal greater than every ordinal in capital X . End proposition. Proof. Let capital Y equals the set of ordinal successors of alpha as alpha ranges over capital X , so that the strict supremum of capital X equals the union of capital Y . Since ordinals are transitive and every member of an ordinal is an ordinal, the strict supremum of capital X is a transitive set of ordinals, and so is an ordinal by corollary four in chapter Ordinals . If alpha belongs to capital X , then the ordinal successor of alpha belongs to capital Y , so the ordinal successor of alpha is a subset of the union of capital Y equals the strict supremum of capital X , and hence alpha belongs to the strict supremum of capital X . So the strict supremum of capital X is strictly greater than every ordinal in capital X . Conversely, if alpha belongs to the strict supremum of capital X , then alpha belongs to the ordinal successor of beta, and that successor belongs to capital Y for some beta belongs to capital X , so that alpha is less than or equal to beta, and beta belongs to capital X . So the strict supremum of capital X is the least strict upper bound on capital X . End proof. End section. End chapter.
